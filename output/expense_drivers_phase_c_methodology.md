# Expense Drivers, Phase C: does headcount respond to growth?

Companion to `expense_drivers_phase_c_ratios.csv`. Plan: `docs/expense_drivers_workplan.md`. Generated by `scripts/expense_drivers_phase_c.py`.

## An elasticity framing was tried and rejected

The first version computed elasticities of FTE to cumulative new growth and got +0.00 everywhere, which looked like a decisive null and was a bug: cumulative new growth runs from one year's worth to fifteen years' worth, a change of **+1,820%**, so any headcount change over it is zero by construction.

The positive control caught it. School staff against enrollment gave **+2.32, +4.55, -13.34 and -3.83** across four windows, because enrollment moves only 6% in fifteen years and an elasticity with a denominator that small is not a stable statistic. A control that changes sign is rejecting the method, not confirming it. Direct physical ratios are reported instead.

## The denominator, checked three ways

| Source | Net new homes per year |

| Implied from new-growth dollars, FY2011-25 | 115 |

They agree on order of magnitude, which is what a ratio quoted to one significant figure needs.

## Results: what a new home buys in staffing

**Every figure here is an upper bound**, because all headcount change is attributed to growth and headcount changes for many other reasons: new services, restructuring, outsourcing, grant cycles. An upper bound that still lands low is the useful result.

## The school prediction did not survive

Phase A expected school staffing to track enrollment. Over FY2011-2025 enrollment rose +6.0% (6,627 to 7,023) while school staff rose +27.2% (982.3 to 1,249.4). Students per staff FTE fell from 6.75 to 5.62. School staffing growth was a service-level choice far more than an enrollment response.

That matters for the fiscal impact model in the same way the town-side result does. A per-pupil marginal cost assumes staffing follows students. Over this period Brookline's staffing moved largely independently of them, which means the per-pupil figure describes an accounting average rather than an observed response.

## What none of this establishes

- **Not causal.** These are ratios of observed changes.
- **Not a null on service cost.** A weak staffing response is consistent with services genuinely not scaling, with productivity absorbing growth, and with service levels quietly falling. Nothing here separates them.
- **Not a refutation of RKG's $1,371.** That figure is a cost allocation, not a prediction about headcount. What Phase C shows is that the staffing channel people usually have in mind when defending it is hard to see in twenty years of Brookline data.
