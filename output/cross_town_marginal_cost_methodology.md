# What a new home adds to town spending: cross-town test, FY2011-2021

Companion to `cross_town_marginal_cost_fy11_21.csv` (results) and
`cross_town_panel_fy11_21.csv` (the 350-town dataset). Generated by
`scripts/cross_town_marginal_cost.py`, which prints its own controls.

## The question

The fiscal model charges $1,371 per new home for non-school town services, from
the Town's consultant RKG. $1,109 of that is department budgets and 88% of the
$1,109 is police ($448) and fire ($532). RKG built it by marking each FY2025
budget line as fixed or incremental and dividing the incremental lines by
occupied households. That is an **average-cost** method: it assumes a new home
needs the same share of police, fire and other services as the average existing
home.

Brookline's own history doesn't show that. Public safety positions fell by 5 over
FY2011-25 while about 1,500 homes were added. One town over one period can't
separate growth from everything else, so this tests the question across every
Massachusetts municipality.

## Data

| Input | Source | Years |
|---|---|---|
| Housing units, population | Census PL 94-171 redistricting files, geographic header (HU100, POP100), county subdivisions | April 2010, April 2020 |
| Department spending (General Government, Public Safety, Public Works, Human Services, Culture & Recreation) | DOR Schedule A, General Fund | FY2011, FY2021 |
| Non-residential new growth value (commercial control; includes personal property) | DOR new growth data | FY2012-2021 |

350 of 351 municipalities matched. Brookline: 26,448 to 27,961 units (+1,513, or
151 a year, consistent with the Planning Department's count of about 166 a year).

## Results, in FY2025 dollars per added housing unit

| Specification | All five departments | Public safety only |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Growth rates, each town one observation** (preferred) | **$663** [&minus;$383 to $1,599] | **$234** [&minus;$1,220 to $1,131] |
| Dollars per existing unit (towns count comparably) | $185 [&minus;$1,018 to $1,185] | $297 [&minus;$16 to $841] |
| Dollars, unweighted | $2,837 [$718 to $5,390] | $1,373 [&minus;$94 to $2,529] |
| Dollars, unweighted, without Boston | $2,622 [$492 to $5,346] | $514 [&minus;$278 to $1,701] |
| RKG, for comparison | $1,109 | $980 |

Brackets are 95% intervals from a 2,000-draw town-level bootstrap.

The preferred form estimates an elasticity: across towns, a 10% increase in
housing goes with a **2.2%** increase in department spending (95% range &minus;1.3%
to +5.3%), holding commercial growth and the common statewide growth rate fixed.
An elasticity of 1 would mean spending rises in proportion to housing, which is
what average costing assumes. Multiplying by Brookline's FY2021 department
spending per unit ($2,544) gives the dollar figure.

**The unweighted dollar forms are not used.** They are driven by a handful of
large cities (Boston, Worcester, Cambridge, Quincy) that added the most units and
also grew their budgets 30-60% for many other reasons. Dropping Boston alone
barely moves them because the next few cities carry the same pattern. The forms
that give each town comparable weight agree with each other at roughly $200-650
per unit.

## Controls

1. A known $2,000 per unit injected into every town's spending change is
   recovered exactly.
2. Shuffling housing growth across towns drops the estimate to about $140 per
   unit, against $2,400 in the unweighted real data, so the specification can
   find a relationship when one exists.

## What it means

- **The central estimate is below RKG, about 60% of it**, and the interval runs
  from zero to about 1.4 times it. This data can't rule RKG out; it makes it the
  high end of a plausible range rather than the central figure.
- **Public safety is where the gap is widest**: about $230 per unit against RKG's
  $980, with a wide interval. That matches Brookline's own record of public safety
  staff not rising with housing.
- **This fits the fiscal impact literature.** Average costing suits towns with
  steady growth where services expand in step with demand. In built-out
  communities with existing capacity, infill development uses existing service
  patterns, so the cost of the next unit is usually below average (Kotval and
  Mullin, *Fiscal Impact Analysis: Methods, Cases, and Intellectual Debate*,
  Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, 2006). Brookline adds about 0.5% to its housing
  a year.

## Limits

- A cross-section, not an experiment. Towns that add housing differ in wealth,
  density and growth pressure, and those can move spending too.
- Ten years. Costs that arrive in steps, like a new fire company or patrol shift
  once workload crosses a threshold, may not show up within one decade in a given
  town, even though they are real over a longer horizon.
- Schedule A function categories are reported by each town and are not perfectly
  consistent across towns.
- Non-residential new growth includes personal property, so it is a rough
  commercial control.
