Brookline's finances to 2040

Where the Town's General Fund is headed, what decides it, and how you can test every assumption yourself.

Brookline's finances can stay healthy through 2040. A few recurring decisions matter more than anything else, and new building makes the picture stronger, not weaker.

What the forecast finds

Five findings, each with the chart behind it. Each links to a short read with the detail.

  1. How Brookline got here

    Inflation, not growth, drove spending

    Inflation was {{fact:cause_pct_inflation}} of the growth in General Fund spending from FY2011 to FY2025. More staff, mostly in the schools, was another {{fact:cause_pct_staffing_levels}}, and debt for new buildings, mostly schools, another {{fact:cause_pct_buildings_debt_service}}. Growth in homes and commercial space added about {{fact:cause_pct_new_homes_commercial_space}}.

    The same staff doing the same work cost more each year, in pay, prices and health premiums. The revenue Brookline controls kept pace with that; state aid did not, and the overrides filled most of the gap.

    Read: How Brookline got here

  2. Where the budget is headed

    The override keeps the budget ahead to 2040

    The override helped. Counting it, revenue covers spending every year to 2040 in the base case, with room of {{fact:room_2030s_low}} to {{fact:room_2030s_high}} a year through the 2030s and {{fact:room_fy2040}} in FY2040.

    The base case continues how costs and revenue have behaved since FY2011: school staff fall about half as fast as enrollment, health premiums rise 12% a year through FY2029 and then 8%, and Brookline adds {{fact:homes_pace}} homes a year. Once the pension money has paid off the retiree health liability, in {{fact:opeb_paid_off}}, the base case sets it aside for a decision rather than counting it as room. FY2028 is the thinnest year, with {{fact:room_fy2028}} of room. The room is capacity for the decisions below, not a surplus that piles up.

    Read: Where the budget is headed

  3. Growth

    Judge growth town-wide, by capacity

    Construction adds revenue predictably, about two years after its permits. Costs have not followed new homes; they rise when a service runs out of room, and by some measures there is room today.

    From FY2011 to FY2025 permits added {{fact:homes_permitted_fy11_25}} homes while police and fire staffing fell by five positions, and enrollment followed births, not new buildings. Costs step up when a grade fills or the town needs a new fire company, which depends on everything being built, not any one project. Those measures of room still need checking with the people who run the services.

    Read: Growth, what it adds and what it costs

  4. The decisions that matter

    Four decisions matter most

    Four recurring decisions matter more than anything else: how fast pay grows, what the health plan costs, school staffing as enrollment falls, and what to do with the money freed when the pension liability is paid off. Each is worth tens of millions a year by 2040.

    None of them requires closing a program. Most are about what the Town pays for the services it already provides.

  5. What to watch

    A few readings show what's coming

    Births, permits, open seats, and pay and health costs show whether the forecast is holding, some of them years ahead, so plans can adjust early. The first two are already in: building permits show less construction than expected in the next few years, and births remain well below what the School Department's forecast assumes.

    Permits issued in 2024 and 2025 point to FY2028 new growth well under what the base case assumes. Each year's levy builds on the last, so that one shortfall carries forward: even if growth returns to its usual pace afterward, FY2040 would have {{fact:pl_effect_fy2040}} less room. Births of {{fact:ind_births}} in {{fact:ind_births_year}}, against {{fact:ind_births_cm}} a year in the School Department's forecast, point to enrollment that keeps falling. Fire and medical calls are among the other readings.

    Read: What to watch

Why this differs from the Town's plan

The two answer different questions: a budget plan is deliberately cautious, and this asks what happens if costs and revenue keep behaving as they have. The Town's long-range plan, presented in January 2026, shows a gap of about $43 million in FY2031; this forecast shows about {{fact:room_fy2031}} of room that year. Most of the difference is the override, which passed four months after the plan was written and is worth about $27 million a year by FY2031, and school pay. The Town's plan uses the School Department's forecast of about 5% growth per year; this one uses about 3.7%, what the current contract's 2.75% raises plus step increases work out to by the School Department's own arithmetic. Every step between the two is laid out in the base case chapter.

About this work

This is an independent analysis by Ben Chostner, a Town Meeting Member for Precinct 9. It is not an official Town document. It is built from the Town's published budgets, financial plans and assessment records, School Department budget documents, state data and enrollment forecasts, and every figure can be traced to its source.
