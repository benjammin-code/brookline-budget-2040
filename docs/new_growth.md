# New homes and Brookline's budget

> **Enrollment in Brookline's public schools is expected to keep falling. If the Town keeps
> its school staff and classrooms at today's levels, as the School Department plans, new
> homes are among the cheapest ways to add revenue between now and 2040.**

## Summary

- **By the schools' own class-size guidelines, there are open seats, and more are coming.** There are 6,833 students this
  fall, down from 7,855 in 2019, and the number is expected to fall to {{fact:enroll_fy2040_about}} by 2040,
  mainly because fewer children are being born in town. This year's kindergarten through
  grade 8 classes already have about 1,100 empty seats under the School Committee's own
  class-size limits, and about 440 with every class kept three students below them.
- **The School Department plans to keep today's teachers and classes, even as enrollment
  falls.** So those empty seats will stay open unless something fills them.
- **Police and fire staffing has not grown with housing.** Brookline's building permits
  added {{fact:homes_permitted_fy11_25}} homes over the last fifteen years, and the number of police officers and firefighters
  did not go up. That may mean the departments had room, or that the same staff stretched
  further; the published figures cannot tell which, and the departments have not been asked.
- **That changes what a new home costs the Town.** The usual estimate says a typical new
  apartment roughly breaks even, because it assumes every new student needs new teachers.
  If new students fill seats that are already empty, the same home brings the Town about
  $1,700 a year more than it costs.
- **On paper, the seats could take a lot of homes.** On top of the {{fact:homes_pace}} a year
  Brookline already builds, the empty classroom seats could take the children from {{fact:seats_homes_fy2030}} more
  homes by 2030 and {{fact:seats_homes_fy2040}} more by 2040. In practice services usually
  strain before seats or staff run out, in a particular school or grade, in special education
  space, or through overtime and turnover, and where those limits lie is not known.
- **The Town has to choose.** It can keep those classes and fill them, or combine classes
  as enrollment falls and save money. It cannot do both. If it keeps them, as the School
  Department plans, new homes are among the cheapest ways to add revenue between now and
  2040.
- **This depends on enrollment falling.** The School Department's own forecast expects
  enrollment to rise again from 2029, to about 7,400 by 2034. That would take more children
  moving into Brookline, for every child born here, than in any year of the last two
  decades, including the boom of the 2010s, at a time when fewer children are being born in
  town. If it does happen, the empty seats fill on their own and the advantage goes away.
  [Enrollment: three forecasts compared](/forecast/topics/enrollment) sets the School
  Department's forecast beside NESDEC's and the base case.

## The usual math

[Growth: when a new home pays for itself](/forecast/topics/growth) sets out the usual test of when a home covers its costs. This page asks what changes
when enrollment is falling and the schools already have empty seats.

The usual way to estimate what a new home costs the Town compares the property tax it pays
with the cost of the Town services its residents use and of educating its children. For a
new apartment in a large building, the kind of home Brookline has mostly been adding, assessed
at about $434,000, it comes out roughly even:

- **Revenue to the Town:** about $4,400 a year in property tax.
- **Expenses to the Town:** about $5,000 a year in total:
    - Town services, about $866 a year (police, fire, public works and the rest, with staff
      benefits);
    - schools, about $4,100 a year: on average 0.244 school-age children per home, each
      costing about {{fact:student_cost}} a year.
- **Net:** about $500 a year short when the home opens.

The forecast's average new home, a mix of apartments and condominiums that adds about
{{fact:homes_value}} of assessed value, pays more tax and comes out about $1,000 a year ahead.

This site's main forecast, the base case, also uses this method for new homes, with one
difference: it applies it only to homes built faster than Brookline's recent pace of
{{fact:homes_pace}} a year, because the children from homes at that pace are already in the enrollment
forecast it uses. The {{fact:student_cost}} is what the base
case charges for each added student in FY2027: about $12,100 for added teachers, aides
and other staff at today's ratio (about one position for every 8.3 students in the parts
of the budget that move with enrollment), about $2,500 for supplies, transport and other
costs that move with each student, and about $2,300 for the added staff's health insurance
and payroll costs. RKG Associates, the Town's consultant, builds its figure the same way
($15,572 a student in FY2025, from the two-thirds of the school budget that moves with
enrollment), and grown to FY2027 it is about the same. Both assume that every added student
brings added staff. That fits a
growing school system, and Brookline's enrollment is falling.

## Schools: fewer students than the classrooms can hold

The schools were crowded a decade ago, and the buildings expanded since then were sized for a
peak that has passed. Brookline's public schools peaked at 7,855 students in FY2019. This fall (2026) there are 6,889.
Births to Brookline residents have fallen from about 690 a year in 2008–13 and about 590 in
2015–19 to 422 in 2023, 443 in 2024 and 423 in 2025.
NESDEC's enrollment forecast, which the base case uses, updated for those births (NESDEC had
estimated 518 and 507), has enrollment falling to {{fact:enroll_fy2040_about}} by FY2040.

{{chart:enroll-seats}}

**Why the base case does not use the School Department's forecast.** The School
Department plans with a 2023 forecast by Cropper-McKibben, the orange dashed line. It has been
closer than NESDEC on total enrollment for the last two years, though NESDEC has been closer for
kindergarten through grade 8, and it has enrollment rising from FY2029 to
about 7,400 by FY2034. In the report's own words, the rise comes from new apartments, older
owners selling homes to families, and young households continuing to move in; it assumes
about 1,500 new homes from 2020 to 2034, fewer than Brookline's recent pace. The base case
uses NESDEC because its decline follows births. The children who will start kindergarten
through fall 2030 are already born, and births have stayed low. The School Department's forecast assumes more births than have been
counted, about 600 a year. If births stay near the counted level, reaching about 7,400 would take
more families moving in, for every child born here, than at any time in the last twenty years. If the School Department's forecast
is right, enrollment passes the FY2027 level by FY2029 even without extra homes, and
the usual math applies. Brookline draws fewer young families than the suburbs mainly because fewer of its homes are
family-sized; for each family-sized home it has about as many school-age children as Lexington
([Why families settle elsewhere](/forecast/topics/families)).

The schools are not being staffed down to match. The School Department's current plan holds
school staffing at the FY2027 level, and this school year's classes already have room. (The base
case lets school staff fall half as fast as enrollment, as they did from FY2019 to FY2025, which
still leaves seats opening each year.) The School Department's October 1 count has 4,436
students in kindergarten through grade 8 this fall, in 236 classes. Against the School
Committee's own class-size guidelines (22 in kindergarten through grade 2, 25 in grades 3–8,
lower at Pierce and Driscoll), those classes have about 1,100 empty seats, and no grade at any
school is over its guideline. The guideline is a ceiling, and this fall's classes average 19.
Kept three students below the guideline, the same classes have about 440 empty seats:

Holding staff flat as enrollment falls also changes the ratio of students to staff. In
FY2019 there were 5.97 students for every school staff member; in FY2027 there are 5.50;
with staff held at the FY2027 level and enrollment at NESDEC's projection, there would be
4.38 by FY2040, fewer students per staff member than in any year since the series began
in FY2008. (These ratios count all school staff. The one position for every 8.2 students
above counts only the two-thirds of staff that the base case treats as moving with
enrollment: 5.50 ÷ 0.67 ≈ 8.2.) Across all of Brookline's roughly 29,000 homes, public school students per home
fall from about 0.24 today to about 0.18 by FY2040. The falling ratio is another measure
of the same empty seats.

The rest of this page leaves out the 1,100 seats already open and counts only the seats the
decline adds: how many students could arrive before enrollment is back to the FY2027
level. That number grows each year, from about {{fact:seats_students_fy2030}} students in FY2030 to about
{{fact:seats_students_fy2040}} in FY2040.

## Town services: police and fire have not grown with homes

Brookline's building permits added {{fact:homes_permitted_fy11_25}} homes from FY2011 to FY2025, and the Town's public
safety staffing fell by five positions over the same years. Fire staffing is set by
stations and companies, each staffed around the clock, so it changes in steps rather than
with each home; Brookline budgeted 161 firefighters from FY2010 to FY2026 and 160 in FY2027, and it has more
firefighters per home than the median of 21 nearby cities and towns, and about the median number
of police officers per home.

RKG Associates, the Town's consultant, assumes a new home costs the Town $1,371 a year in
new expenses for Town services, not counting schools. It gets there by
dividing the parts of each department budget it judges to grow with households evenly
across existing homes, so a new home is assumed to need the same share of police and fire
as an average one; 88% of its department figure is police and fire. That is an average
cost, and Brookline's own record does not show police and fire growing that way.

The base case instead uses $660 a year in FY2025 dollars ($866 with benefits in FY2027),
measured from what actually happened: across 350 Massachusetts towns from FY2011 to FY2021,
10% more homes went with about 2.2% more department spending. About {{fact:ps_share_per_home}} of the $660 is
public safety. The base case leaves fire's part of that, about {{fact:ps_share_fire_per_home}},
out and counts fire in steps instead: a fire company of 20 firefighters once the town reaches
about {{fact:fire_company_homes}} homes, which at the recent pace of building comes after
FY2040. Police keep their part, about {{fact:ps_share_police_per_home}} a home, so police cost
grows a little with every new home. Police calls have fallen since FY2016, and Brookline's
officers per home are close to the median of nearby cities and towns, so there is no clear point
at which police would step up the way a fire company does. The rest of the $660 covers the other ways more households add to department
spending, which in the towns studied rose across departments, not only in public safety.
Given Brookline's own record, it probably overstates what a new home adds to the cost of
Town services. Under either figure,
Town services cost far less per home than schools.

Flat staffing is not proof of room, though. It could also mean the same officers and
firefighters covering more residents, and the figures here come from budgets and reports,
not from the people who run those departments. [New homes and the budget: is there
room?](/forecast/topics/growth-links#what-these-measures-cannot-see) sets out what the
published measures can and cannot show, and what to ask.

## Net financial impact of a new home when its children fill empty seats

**At the forecast's average of 0.244 school-age children per home, every type of new home
pays for itself when its children fill empty seats.** Under the usual estimate, all but
the lowest-value rental apartments do too.

If a new home's children join classes that are already running, the added cost is not a
share of a new teacher. It is the supplies, textbooks and computers that grow with each seat,
about $670 a student in FY2027, plus special education at the district average, about
$7,760 in all. [Schools: staffing, costs
and open seats](/forecast/topics/schools#three-tiers-for-the-cost-of-an-added-student) has the breakdown. Here is what each type of home brings the Town
each year after its costs, both ways:

{{fact:homes_value}}, is the value the forecast's new homes add: apartments and condominiums in their
2016–2025 proportions, measured by following recent projects onto the assessment rolls
([Counting Brookline's homes](/forecast/topics/homes)). Tax at FY2025 values and the FY2026

two-families.*

Filling empty seats, even the lowest-value apartments would need about 0.46 school-age
children per home before they stopped paying for themselves, nearly twice the forecast's
0.244. Brookline's recent developments range from about 0.03 to 0.56 and average about 0.21.

## Keep the classes, or combine them

Empty seats are not free. The Town is paying for classes with room in them, and it could
instead combine classes as enrollment falls and save money. The base case forecast already lets
school staff fall half as fast as enrollment; holding them at the FY2027 level instead would cost
{{fact:fy40_staff_keep_abs}} a year by FY2040, and letting them follow enrollment fully would add
about {{fact:fy40_staff_follow_abs}}.
The same seats can be cut or filled, not both, so decisions about housing and about school
staffing are linked. If Brookline keeps its classes, as the School Department plans, new
homes are among the cheapest ways to add revenue between now and 2040. If it combines
classes to match falling enrollment, the usual math applies again: a new apartment roughly
breaks even and the forecast's average new home comes out a little ahead.

## Limits

- **These are measures from the outside.** Open seats, staffing and call counts come from
  budgets and reports. The people who run the schools and the police and fire departments
  have not been asked for this page whether they have room, and they may see it differently.
  Services often strain before any measure says they are full.
- **Seats are in specific schools and grades.** Ruffin Ridley and Pierce are fuller than
  the rest, and Pierce's guidelines reflect space limits at its Fisher Hill site. A large
  building's children all arrive in one school's district.
- **Children per home varies.** The 0.244 average hides a range of about 0.03 to 0.56 in
  recent Brookline buildings; tenure and bedroom count decide where a project lands.
- **Special education costs do not follow seats.** About 18% of Brookline students have an
  IEP, and some services and out-of-district placements cost far more than a seat.
- **The enrollment forecast could be wrong.** If enrollment follows the School
  Department's forecast instead of NESDEC's, there are no empty seats to fill from
  FY2029.
- **Step costs.** A new fire company, a new school building or a bus route arrives all at
  once. The forecast models one such step, a fire company at about {{fact:fire_company_homes}}
  homes, which the base case does not reach before 2040; it does not model a new school
  building or bus route.

## Other kinds of new growth

Most of Brookline's new growth is not new homes. Renovation of existing homes is about
half of it, and commercial property and business equipment make up most of the rest. Those
add tax with little or no added cost. [Will Brookline need another
override?](/forecast/budget-to-2040#growth-helps-and-its-kind-matters) shows what each kind of
growth is worth by 2040, and how much faster growth would change the answer.
