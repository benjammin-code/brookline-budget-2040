3A

Where the baseline trajectory leads through 2040

Part 2 defined “sustainable” precisely: not whether actual revenue matches actual expenses each year (Massachusetts towns can’t run a real deficit — that’s always forced into balance by law), but whether automatic revenue — 2.5% a year plus new growth, no override — grows fast enough on its own to keep pace with real cost growth. Run that question forward through 2040, and the answer depends entirely on which assumptions get used — the single biggest finding of this section, ahead of any individual number.

This project builds two forecasts side by side: town_assumptions takes the Town’s own currently-published FY2026–2031 planning figures and mechanically extends them to FY2040 with no changes. trend_corrected replaces those figures, category by category, with this project’s own reconstruction from real trailing data — the real 2024 pension actuarial valuation (not the Financial Plan’s still-cited 2022 one), a full OPEB liability roll-forward, and the Town’s own real CIP/Free Cash funding policy applied to its own real six-year table. Debt exclusion nets to exactly $0 every year in both — this is entirely an operating-budget story.

Two forecasts of the same town — the gap between them is entirely about which assumptions are used, not any new lever

That is a genuinely large gap between two forecasts of the same town, worth being precise about: neither scenario assumes any new lever — no faster new growth, no expense cuts, no future override beyond the real one already passed in May 2026. The entire difference comes from which set of underlying assumptions is more accurate right now. Put plainly: getting the assumptions right closes most of this gap on its own, before any policy lever is even considered — which makes the size of the real gap itself the first finding for 3b to work from, not a settled fact to build a lever menu on top of unchallenged.

The two forecasts agree on one thing precisely: the Town’s own currently-published planning figures, taken at face value and extended with no changes, do not describe a sustainable trajectory. Whether that’s a $146 million problem or something closer to already-solved depends on which picture is closer to right — and either way, what follows is about what happens next, on top of whichever baseline turns out correct.

3B

A menu of levers to close the gap

However large the real remaining gap turns out to be, closing it is not a single decision. This project takes that advice from two sources that don’t usually get cited together: the ERSC Final Report’s own explicit caution against point estimates, and the Town’s own 2030 Roadmap (approved by the Select Board May 19, 2026), whose Goal 3 — “increase the Town’s fiscal health and stability” — already names three of these four levers as adopted Town strategy, not a menu this project invented from scratch.

Relying on overrides alone

The lever every real historical gap has actually been closed with. This project models what “nothing but overrides, at a realistic cadence” would require: taking town_assumptions’ operating gap as-is, and asking what size override — called roughly every three years, matching the ERSC’s own finding that override intervals have compressed — would bring the budget back to breakeven each time.

Four overrides, each bigger than the last, still not enough

New override called each cycle (bars) vs. the gap that reopens between cycles (line, % of revenue)

FY2029 ($8.1M), FY2032 ($16.8M), FY2035 ($33.6M), FY2038 ($42.1M) — each roughly double the one before, compounding to $113.3 million a year of permanent added levy capacity by FY2040. That’s roughly three times this project’s entire FY2005–2025 real-override total ($38.7M, compounded), raised in about three-quarters of the time. And even with all four, the budget isn’t fully closed by FY2040: two years after the last one, the gap has already reopened to -$32.8 million (-5.0% of revenue). A pure override-reliance strategy doesn’t settle into a steady rhythm — it has to keep accelerating.

None of these four levers is sized to close the whole gap alone, and that’s the point of a menu rather than a recommendation. New growth’s best case is real but modest against a nine-figure annual gap; permitting reform has a real data foundation but isn’t sized to a target yet; expense management’s best current documentation, from the Town’s own dedicated study, tops out under half a million dollars a year; overrides alone still leave a real gap open by 2040 while requiring rapidly escalating individual asks. The honest picture is a combination, sized against whichever baseline turns out closer to right — not a single lever asked to do all the work.
