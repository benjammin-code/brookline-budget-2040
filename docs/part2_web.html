Brookline’s Fiscal Machine

Before asking what to do about the Town’s finances, it helps to understand how the machine actually works — where the money comes from, where it goes, and what happens when nothing gets built.

Extends the whitepaper “How does construction impact Brookline’s town finances?” · Reference document, Part 2 of 3

2A

How a dollar of revenue becomes a dollar of spending, every year

Start with the funds. Not all of Brookline’s money is the same money. The Town keeps several separate pools, and knowing which one is in play makes it much easier to follow any conversation about Town finances. The General Fund — about $440 million a year, roughly 92% of everything the Town spends — is what pays for schools, police and fire, public works, libraries, and every other core Town service. It’s also the subject of this entire project. Two more funds, the Water & Sewer Enterprise Fund and the Golf Course Enterprise Fund, are entirely separate and self-supporting: the fees people pay for water, sewer, and golf cover 100% of what those services cost, with no General Fund money involved either way. A Recreation Revolving Fund works along similar lines for recreation programs, though not quite to the same standard — program fees cover about 83% of its costs in FY2026, with the remaining ~17% coming from the General Fund (the Town’s own figures; see below). And since 2021, Brookline has also collected a Community Preservation Act surcharge — an extra 1% added to property tax bills, legally restricted by state law to open space, historic preservation, community housing, and recreation projects. It cannot be spent on schools, salaries, or anything else in the General Fund, no matter how large a gap the General Fund is running. When someone says “the Town has a surplus” or “the Town needs more money,” it matters a great deal which of these five pools they mean — this piece, like the whitepaper before it, is about the General Fund specifically.

Where the money comes from — and where it goes

About 80% of the General Fund is property taxes — $332.5 million of the $419.6 million the Town expects to collect in FY2026. The rest comes from state aid (roughly $26 million, mostly Chapter 70 school funding and other formula grants Brookline has limited control over), local receipts (about $35 million — motor vehicle excise, building permits, meals and rooms taxes, and similar fees), and smaller transfers from other available funds and free cash. On the way out, FY2026’s budget breaks down as schools (34%, the single largest line), departmental services like police, fire, public works, and libraries (24%), employee benefits (22%), debt service (10%), and other non-departmental costs (10%).

That’s not a coincidence; it’s the law. A debt exclusion is a dollar-for-dollar match between what’s collected and what a specific bond costs that year, by definition (more on this below).

Because property tax is so dominant, and because it is the one major revenue source almost entirely within local control, it’s the piece this project focuses on.

What actually limits how much property tax Brookline can collect: Prop 2½

Massachusetts voters passed a law in 1980 — known as Proposition 2½ — that caps how much a town’s property tax collections can grow in a given year, regardless of what happens to individual property values. This is the single most important mechanic in this entire story, so it’s worth laying out carefully.

The cap applies to a number called the levy limit — the maximum total dollar amount the Town is allowed to raise from property taxes townwide. Each year, that limit is allowed to grow by exactly three things added together:

  • 2.5% of last year’s levy limit, automatically, every year, no vote required. This is where “Proposition 2½” gets its name.
  • “New growth” — the tax value added by anything genuinely new since last year: a newly built house, an addition, a converted commercial space, a subdivided lot. New growth is measured in dollars (the newly added assessed value, taxed at last year’s tax rate) and gets added to the levy limit permanently, on top of the 2.5%.
  • Any override voters have approved that year — a permanent increase to the levy limit that requires a townwide ballot vote. Brookline voters have approved five of these since 2005: $6.2 million (FY2009), $7.7 million (FY2016), $6.6 million (FY2019), $12.0 million (FY2024), and $23.25 million (approved May 2026, phased in over FY2027–2029) — the largest of the five, and nearly double the next-largest.

There is a fourth category of property tax dollars, debt exclusions, that sits entirely outside this cap. When Brookline borrows to pay for a specific capital project (a new school building, for example) and voters approve that specific borrowing at the ballot, the resulting bond payments are added to everyone’s tax bill dollar-for-dollar, on top of the levy limit, for as long as that bond is being paid off — and then they disappear when the bond is retired. Debt exclusions are about $28.6 million of Brookline’s current (FY2026) property tax bill; they don’t compound, they don’t count against the 2.5% cap, and they’re tied to a specific, voter-approved project with a defined end date.

Rising home values, on their own, do not give the Town more money to spend.

Here’s why: the 2.5% cap applies to the total levy limit — the town-wide pot — not to any individual property’s tax bill. If every home in Brookline appreciated by exactly the same percentage in a given year, the total amount the Town is allowed to collect would still only grow by 2.5% (plus new growth, plus any override). Nothing else would change in that scenario, since everyone appreciated equally and everyone’s share of the fixed pot stays the same. In the more realistic case where some properties appreciate faster than others, what happens is a redistribution, not new money: a property that appreciated faster than the town-wide average ends up paying a larger share of that same fixed levy limit, and a property that appreciated more slowly pays a smaller share — even though the total amount the Town collects didn’t change at all. Rising values only change how the existing, capped amount gets divided up among property owners.

That single fact is the hinge this entire project turns on: since market appreciation alone can’t grow what the Town is allowed to collect, there are really only two ways Brookline’s property tax revenue can grow faster than 2.5% a year — asking voters directly for an override, or generating new growth through construction and renovation. Everything about “why does new growth matter” traces back to this one mechanic.

How the tax bill splits between homeowners and businesses

Once the total levy limit is set, the Select Board decides each year how much of it falls on residential property versus commercial, industrial, and personal property (business equipment) combined — a combination the state calls “CIP.” State law caps how far a town can shift the burden toward CIP: a commercial property’s tax rate can be at most 175% of what a single, unclassified rate would be. Brookline’s Select Board has, in practice, pushed this shift almost to its legal limit — the FY2026 shift factor is 1.746, just 0.2 percentage points shy of the 1.75 ceiling — meaning there is essentially no room left to shift more of the burden toward CIP through this particular lever without a change in state law.

Zooming out: the General Fund is one of five Town pools, not the whole picture

Everything above is about the General Fund specifically, and it’s worth being explicit about why: it’s the pool that funds core Town services and the one this whole project models, but it is not the whole of Brookline’s finances. The Town runs five separate funds in total, and — per the Town’s own Financial Plan — they cannot be blended into one another under any circumstances, which matters because conflating them is a real, common source of confusion in public budget conversations. Two of the other four, the Water & Sewer and Golf Enterprise Funds, are paid for by the fees people using those services pay, rather than by property tax — in fact money flows the other way, with all three non-General funds together reimbursing the General Fund about $4.0 million a year for staff benefits and overhead they use. The Recreation Revolving Fund is close but not quite there: in FY2026 program fees cover 83.3% of the Recreation Department’s costs, with the remaining 16.7% coming from the General Fund. And the Community Preservation Fund is a wholly separate 1% property-tax surtax, legally restricted by state law (M.G.L. c.44B) to open space, historic preservation, community housing, and recreation projects — it cannot be redirected to schools or any other General Fund purpose, no matter how large a gap the General Fund is running, and vice versa.

When a resident hears about a surplus, a shortfall, or a rate increase, the honest first question is which of these five pools is actually being discussed.

2B

What happens if nothing gets built: “business as usual”

Now the payoff of Section 2A: what happens to Brookline’s finances if, hypothetically, not a single new unit gets built, not a single building gets renovated, and not a single commercial square foot gets added, for years on end?

The Town’s allowed revenue still grows automatically — 2.5% a year, guaranteed by Prop 2½, no vote needed, no construction required. If costs also grew at 2.5% a year, this would be a complete non-story: revenue and expenses would track each other indefinitely, and nothing in this project would need to exist. That is not what has happened.

Since 2005, Brookline’s total General Fund expenses have grown at roughly 3.92% a year — above the 2.5% a year Prop 2½ allows without new growth or an override. That total is made up of three pieces growing at noticeably different speeds. Employee benefits — pension contributions, retiree health insurance, and active-employee health insurance, all subject to actuarial requirements and market-driven healthcare cost inflation — have grown at roughly 5.5% a year. Education, Brookline’s single largest budget line, has grown at roughly 4.5% a year. Everything else — police and fire, public works, libraries, general government, and debt service, combined — has grown at roughly 2.5% a year, right in line with what Prop 2½ alone allows. So the gap between what’s capped at 2.5% and what actually gets spent comes almost entirely from those first two categories.

Two decades of diverging growth rates

The chart has two solid, thick “hero” lines and three thin dashed reference lines, and they’re answering different questions. Total General Fund revenue (every dollar coming in — property tax, state aid, local receipts, free cash) and total General Fund expenses are the whole-budget comparison, and they roughly should track each other, since Massachusetts towns aren’t allowed to run a structural deficit: by FY2025, revenue is at 232 against expenses at 216 — ahead, but not wildly so, consistent with the Town building some reserve capacity over 20 years rather than running an actual imbalance. The three dashed lines are a narrower, levy-limit-only comparison (all three exclude debt exclusion, which sits outside this mechanic entirely) — the “if capped at 2.5% only” floor, a middle line showing what 2.5% plus only new growth would have produced with zero overrides, and the real, actual levy limit (which did include four overrides in this window). The middle line is the most interesting of the three: 2.5% plus new growth alone — with not one override ever passed — would have grown the levy limit at 3.91%/yr, landing at index 215 by FY2025, almost exactly on top of real total expenses (216). Over the full 20 years, new growth alone did almost as much work as expenses actually required; the four overrides Brookline did pass were real, necessary corrections in specific years where the gap opened up faster than new growth could close it, not evidence that new growth alone was structurally insufficient across the whole window. And property tax overall (the real levy limit including overrides, 248) still runs ahead of total revenue (232) for a separate reason: property tax has grown faster (4.9%/yr) than the Town’s other two major revenue sources over the same 20 years — state aid at just 1.9%/yr and local receipts at 3.1%/yr — so property tax has been carrying a rising share of the total funding mix. Brookline has become more property-tax-dependent over time, not less, because the revenue sources it doesn’t control (state aid especially) haven’t kept pace.

Local receipts specifically are worth a closer look, since “why hasn’t this grown much” has a real, structural answer rather than just noise. Brookline’s own itemized local-receipts filings are only available in the granular DOR format going back to FY2017, not the full 20 years, but that recent window is telling: motor vehicle excise — consistently the single largest, most stable local-receipts category — grew at just 2.6%/yr (FY2017–25), because MA excise tax isn’t assessed on a vehicle’s real market value or indexed to inflation at all; it’s a fixed state depreciation schedule applied to the manufacturer’s list price, so it grows only with the number and mix of registered vehicles, not with prices or inflation the way property assessments do. Fines and forfeitures, another sizable category, actually declined over the same window (−5.4%/yr). Meals tax grew faster (5.5%/yr) but is a small enough slice that it can’t pull the aggregate up much on its own. The general pattern: unlike property tax, most local-receipts categories are flat administrative fees or state-formula-driven excises that don’t move with inflation or property values unless the Town deliberately changes a rate — they need an active decision to grow, not just time passing.

For education specifically, it’s worth asking directly whether that 4.5% a year reflects more students or the same students costing more to teach. The clearest data available covers the past six years rather than the full twenty: district-wide enrollment peaked at 7,777 students just before the pandemic (FY2019–20) and has since fallen to 6,948 (FY2025–26) — an 11% decline — while teaching and staff positions fell by a similar amount (roughly 8%) over the same stretch. In other words, at least for this recent window, school costs kept climbing while both enrollment and staffing were shrinking, which points toward per-student and per-position costs as the driver, not simply more children in the system. A clean enrollment comparison reaching all the way back to 2005 isn’t available yet from the sources this project has pulled together so far; this is a place worth digging further before the final write-up.

Employee benefits tell a related story. The clearest data point available compares Brookline’s own pension system between its two most recent actuarial valuations (January 2022 and January 2024): active employees in the pension system grew from 1,347 to 1,367 (+1.5%), while retirees and beneficiaries already drawing a pension grew from 891 to 929 (+4.3%) — retirees growing nearly three times as fast as active employees, over just those two years. That’s consistent with benefits costs being driven more by a growing, longer-living retiree population and rising per-person healthcare costs than by Brookline simply employing more people. As with enrollment, this is a two-year comparison, not a twenty-year one — a fuller history of both active and retired headcounts would sharpen this further.

That combination — costs outpacing the 2.5% cap, driven mostly by benefits and schools — is what five separate Brookline overrides since 2005 (2009, 2016, 2019, 2024, and most recently May 2026) have gone to close. Simply adding up the five approved amounts gives $55.7 million, but that actually understates what they’re worth today: an override doesn’t just add its own dollar figure once and stop — it permanently raises the levy-limit base, and that larger base then compounds at 2.5% a year forever after, the same as every other dollar in the levy. The 2009 override’s $6.2 million, for instance, has had 16 years to compound at 2.5%/yr and is worth roughly $9.2 million within the FY2025 levy limit; the 2016 and 2019 overrides work out to roughly $9.6 million and $7.6 million respectively. Compounding the four overrides that fall within this project’s FY2005–2025 window forward to FY2025 (the fifth, May 2026’s $23.25 million, hasn’t started compounding yet as of this window’s endpoint) puts their combined current-dollar contribution to the FY2025 levy limit at roughly $38.7 million — about 19% more than the $32.4 million naive sum of just those four, purely from 2.5% compounding over time. The most recent override is a direct, current illustration of the whole dynamic described above: the School Committee’s own FY2027 budget process identified a shortfall driven largely by non-departmental (“fixed”) costs — pensions, health insurance, and other benefits — rising faster than available revenue, and voters approved a three-year, $23.25 million override in response, split roughly $17.9 million for schools and $5.3 million for town departments. An override is the direct, honest way to close this kind of gap: ask voters for permission to raise more, they say yes or no, and the town’s spending capacity moves accordingly. It is also the lever this project’s baseline forecast deliberately does not assume — every projection in this project that reaches out to 2040 assumes zero future overrides, precisely so that anyone reading the results can see clearly how large a gap remains to be closed by some combination of real levers, override included, rather than having an assumed future override already baked invisibly into the baseline.

This is not a crisis and it is not mismanagement — it is the mechanical, predictable consequence of a state law that caps tax growth at a flat rate while healthcare costs and school budgets do not grow at a flat rate.

This is “business as usual”: a structural, recurring gap between what Prop 2½ automatically allows and what services actually cost to run, closed periodically and directly by asking voters. The only question is which combination of levers closes the gap going forward, and that is exactly what the rest of this project, and Part 3 in particular, is about.

What does “sustainable” actually mean here?

It’s worth being precise about this, because two different comparisons are easy to conflate. Actual total revenue and actual total expenses don’t need a gap between their growth rates — they’re required to balance every single year, by law. Massachusetts towns cannot run a structural deficit; whatever a year’s appropriations are, that year’s revenue (property tax, state aid, local receipts, and any free cash drawn down) has to cover them. So the fact that the “total revenue” and “total expenses” lines in the chart above track each other fairly closely isn’t a target being hit — it’s a legal requirement being satisfied, year by year, using whatever levers (an override, a free cash draw, a mid-year cut) it takes to make it true.

The comparison that actually matters for “sustainable” is narrower: does the revenue the Town gets automatically — 2.5% plus new growth, with no override and no vote — grow fast enough to keep up with real cost growth on its own? If it does, the budget balances itself indefinitely without asking voters for more. If it doesn’t, the gap has to be closed some other way, year after year, and since free cash and reserves are finite, that eventually means either an override, cutting services, or generating more new growth. The dashed lines above show this isn’t hypothetical: 2.5% plus the new growth Brookline actually generated would have grown the levy limit at almost exactly the same rate total expenses grew (3.91% vs. 3.92%) — nearly sustainable on its own, over the full 20 years, with room for it to have gone either way in any given year, which is exactly why real overrides were still needed at specific points along the way even though the 20-year averages came out this close. Whether that holds going forward depends heavily on whether new growth keeps pace with whichever expense categories are running hottest — recently, that’s employee benefits at 5.5%/yr, well above the blended 20-year expense average, which is why Part 3’s scenario menu treats “how much new growth” as a real, quantifiable lever rather than a vague aspiration.

2C

What construction actually does — to both sides of the ledger

Section 2A established that new construction and renovation are one of only two ways (the other being an override) that Brookline’s allowed property tax revenue can grow faster than 2.5% a year. This section makes that concrete: what does a real construction project actually do to the Town’s finances, and why does the kind of project — not just its total dollar value — matter so much to the answer?

The revenue side is straightforward. When a new unit is built, an existing building is renovated, or commercial space is added or upgraded, the Assessor eventually captures that added value, and it becomes “new growth” — permanently added to the levy limit, on top of the 2.5%, no vote required. This is genuinely new revenue capacity for the Town, not a redistribution of an existing capped pool the way ordinary market appreciation is. Since 2005, this mechanism has added real, measurable capacity to Brookline’s budget every single year — but, per the whitepaper’s original finding, at only about 1.3% of the prior levy per year on average, well below what peer communities like Cambridge, Boston, Needham, and Lexington have generated (2.5%+ each), and below the 80th-percentile statewide rate of roughly 2.0%.

“Permanently” is doing real work in that last paragraph, and it’s worth making concrete. New growth doesn’t just add its dollar value once, the year it’s detected — like an override (see the compounding note above), it becomes part of the base going forward, and that base compounds at 2.5% a year forever after, exactly like every other dollar in the levy. A modest amount of new growth in one year is a small number by itself; the same amount, compounding for a decade or two, isn’t. Brookline’s real FY2006 new growth, for example, added $2.23 million to that year’s levy limit — a routine, unremarkable-looking figure at the time. Left alone to compound at 2.5%/yr for the 19 years since, that single year’s addition alone is worth $3.57 million within the FY2025 levy limit — 60% more than its original dollar value, from compounding alone, on top of it having permanently expanded the Town’s spending capacity every year in between. Multiply that by 20 years of annual new-growth additions, each compounding from its own start date, and the cumulative effect is exactly what the “levy limit, 2.5% + new growth only” line in Section 2B’s chart shows — a small, steady annual input producing a large cumulative difference by the end.

The expense side is where it gets more interesting, and where the type of project matters enormously. A new resident, especially a school-age child, costs the Town real money — primarily through the schools, which is by far the largest marginal cost driver of new residential growth (using the RKG Associates figures behind Brookline’s own tax classification hearings and the ERSC report, a marginal cost of roughly $15,600–$26,700 per additional pupil per year, plus a smaller per-unit municipal service cost). A new commercial employee costs the Town comparatively little, since commercial space generates no school enrollment at all and requires a much smaller marginal service footprint per employee. This asymmetry — students are expensive, employees are cheap, from the Town’s perspective — is the single biggest reason why “how much got built” is the wrong question on its own, and “what kind of project got built, at what value” is the right one.

The honest, unifying takeaway is not “residential growth is bad” or “commercial growth is good” — it’s that net fiscal impact depends on the value and physical mix of what gets built, not simply on how much gets built, and that a town-wide policy conversation about encouraging new growth needs to be precise about which kind of growth it’s encouraging, because the two ends of that spectrum produce genuinely different outcomes for the Town’s bottom line.

That 1.3 point-per-year gap, sustained for two decades, came entirely from new growth — not from existing owners’ pockets. Run the same model forward to 2040 under Brookline’s current pace of new growth, and that gap persists (existing owners’ bills projected to grow 2.34% a year against 3.72% townwide); hit the peer-town growth rate Part 3 will discuss, and the gap widens further, to existing owners growing at 2.30% against a townwide 4.36%. This is the mechanical result of everything in Section 2A: new growth adds genuinely new revenue capacity on top of the capped levy limit, so more of it means existing residents are asked to cover proportionally less of the Town’s growing costs, not more.
