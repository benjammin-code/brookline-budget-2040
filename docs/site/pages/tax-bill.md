## How a tax bill is set

Brookline decides how much it may collect in total, and that total is shared among every
property in town in proportion to its [[assessed value]]. Under [[Proposition 2½]] the total can
grow about 2.5% a year, plus [[new growth]] and any [[override]] or [[debt exclusion]] voters
approve. So your bill changes for three reasons:

- **The town's total changes: your bill goes up, or occasionally down.** The 2.5% a year,
  overrides and new debt exclusions all raise it. It falls only when a debt exclusion is paid off.
- **New property arrives: your bill goes down, slightly.** [[New growth]] is taxed on its own
  value, so it raises the total without raising anyone else's bill. It does help with debt
  exclusions, which are fixed dollar amounts: the more property there is, the smaller each home's
  share of the same debt.
- **Your value moves differently from everyone else's: your bill goes up or down.** If your
  home's value rises faster than the town's, your share, and your bill, rises; if it rises more
  slowly, your bill falls behind.

Rising values on their own do not raise the total. When every home gains 10%, the tax rate falls
to match, and the total stays the same.

Owners who live in their homes get the [[residential exemption]], which takes a fixed amount off
each home's assessed value. Rental property does not. An average apartment in a building of four
or more units was assessed at {{fact:bill_apt_value_fy2026}} in FY2026 and carried
{{fact:bill_apt_fy2026}} of tax, about {{fact:bill_apt_month_fy2026}} a month: more than the median
owner-occupied condominium, on a lower value. The landlord pays it. Rents are set by the market rather
than by the tax bill, but over time the tax is one of the costs rents have to cover.

The figures on this page leave out the Community Preservation Act surcharge, which voters approved
in 2021 for open space, historic preservation, affordable housing and recreation. It is 1% of the
tax after the residential exemption, about {{fact:bill_cpa_sf_fy2026}} on the median single-family
bill in FY2026, with exemptions for lower-income owners. Because it moves with the bill, adding it
would raise every figure here by about 1% without changing the comparisons.

## What has happened since FY2018

From FY2018 to FY2026 the median single-family bill rose about 7.3% a year, from $11,882 to
$20,904. The median condominium bill rose about 2.9% a year, from $3,933 to $4,963. Three things
drove that:
the [[override|overrides]] voted in 2018 and 2023; debt exclusions for the High School, Driscoll, Pierce, the Newbury
College land and the fire stations; and single-family values rising much faster than condominium values (about 6.0% a
year against 3.0%), which shifted tax from condominiums to single-family homes. These figures are the
Town's own medians, which include the [[residential exemption]] for owner-occupied homes.

FY2018 to FY2026 single-family values rose about 6.0% a year and condominium values 3.0%, which moved tax from

shown here, and condominium and apartment bills more slowly.

Most of the base case's increase comes early: the May 2026 override adds its three steps by
FY2029, so a typical bill grows about {{fact:bill_base_cagr_26_30}} a year to FY2030. After that it
grows about {{fact:bill_base_cagr_30_40}} a year, a little less than 2.5%, because existing debt
exclusion payments are spread over a growing town. Those payments stay about level. The Town's debt
schedule raises them from $35.4 million in FY2027 to about $36.7 million in FY2029, as borrowing
voters have already approved begins, and holds them there through FY2033, the last year it shows. Beyond
that, the Town's bond disclosures show payments on all of its existing debt falling by less than
$3 million between FY2033 and FY2040, so at most a small part of the excluded debt is paid off
before FY2040; most of it runs to the mid-2040s, and the Pierce and fire station bonds to 2050. The
forecast keeps the payments level to FY2040 and adds no new debt exclusions.

The Baker example raises growth after FY2030 from {{fact:bill_base_cagr_30_40}} to
{{fact:bill_baker_cagr_30_40}} a year. It adds about {{fact:bill_baker_sf_extra_fy2035}} a year to the median
single-family bill and about {{fact:bill_baker_condo_extra_fy2035}} to the median condominium
bill. Faster new growth saves the median single-family home about
{{fact:bill_growth_sf_saving_fy2040}} a year by FY2040. That is small for any one household, but
it is the only one of the four paths that lowers bills while also giving the Town more to spend. Its larger effect is on whether another override is needed, which
depends on what gets built: see [what commercial building does to the budget](@budget-to-2040#when-the-room-runs-out).

## How sure this is

This is a projection for a home whose value keeps pace with the town average, and it assumes every
type of home gains value at the same rate. An individual bill can rise faster or slower if that
home's value does, as single-family bills did from FY2018 to FY2026. The residential exemption is assumed to keep its current share of value. The paths use
the base case's revenue forecast, so anything that changes the town's total tax, such as
overrides or new debt exclusions not shown here, would change these figures. The
[Forecast Explorer](@explorer) shows how the budget, rather than any one bill, responds to each
assumption.
