question: Why does Brookline draw fewer young families than towns like Lexington?
answer: Not because its births fell faster, and not simply because it costs more. Brookline's births fell about as much as Lexington's. What sets the family suburbs apart is how many of their homes are family-sized, with three or more bedrooms. Fewer than half of Brookline's homes are, against three in four in Lexington, and for each home of that size the two towns have about the same number of school-age children.

The base case assumes Brookline keeps drawing young families at about its recent rate. The
School Department's forecast expects more births, about 600 a year against the
{{fact:ind_births}} counted in {{fact:ind_births_year}}. If births stay near the counted level,
its FY2034 kindergarten would need about {{fact:cm_kpb_fy2034}} kindergartners for every birth,
more than Newton or Belmont draw today. This page looks at why towns differ in how many families
they draw, using public data for Brookline and eleven towns it is usually compared with.

Throughout, a **family-sized home** means one with three or more bedrooms. Two sources count them.
The Census Bureau's American Community Survey counts bedrooms in every home, rented or owned.
Town assessment records cover houses and condominiums but not the units inside apartment
buildings, and record rooms rather than bedrooms; six or more rooms matched three or more bedrooms
in about nine Brookline homes out of ten.

mix, not a count of where children live. Somerville's low figure is a reminder that larger
apartments there are often shared by adults.

## What goes with drawing families

are family-sized, from assessment records.

Across the twelve towns the two move closely together (a correlation of
{{fact:corr_kpb_family_share}}), and the Census Bureau's own count of homes with three or more
bedrooms tells the same story ({{fact:corr_kpb_acs_3br}}). Price does much less to separate them
({{fact:corr_kpb_price}}): Wellesley's family-sized homes cost more than Brookline's and it still
draws families, while Somerville's cost far less and it does not.

Brookline's housing is mostly smaller condominiums and apartments. About
{{fact:family_share_brookline}} of its houses and condos are family-sized, against
{{fact:family_share_lexington}} in Lexington, and counting apartments too the shares are
{{fact:big_share_brookline}} and {{fact:big_share_lexington}}. In the Census Bureau's 2020–24
survey 23.5% of Brookline's homes were single-family houses, against 82.6% in Lexington. That housing suits the people who
live there: 18.8% of Brookline residents are 25 to 34, against 4.2% in Lexington, and
{{fact:hh_children_brookline}} of households have children, against {{fact:hh_children_lexington}}.

The family-sized homes Brookline does have are expensive, even by the standard of its neighbors.
Family-sized homes that sold in 2023 to 2025 went for a median of
{{fact:family_price_brookline}} in Brookline and {{fact:family_price_lexington}} in Lexington. A
six-room house in Brookline sold for a median of $2.54 million, against $1.66 million in
Lexington, and a six-room condominium for $1.54 million, which is about what a typical Lexington
house costs.

## Brookline has been expensive for a long time

The state's figures for the average single-family value show that Brookline did not become
expensive recently. From FY2010 to FY2026 the average rose {{fact:sfvalue_growth_brookline}} in
Brookline, {{fact:sfvalue_growth_lexington}} in Lexington, {{fact:sfvalue_growth_newton}} in Newton
and {{fact:sfvalue_growth_wellesley}} in Wellesley. Brookline's average was
{{fact:sfvalue_ratio_fy2010}} times Lexington's in FY2010 and {{fact:sfvalue_ratio_fy2026}} times
in FY2026 ({{fact:sfvalue_brookline_fy2026}} against {{fact:sfvalue_lexington_fy2026}}). The gap in
dollars has widened as all values rose, but Brookline has not pulled away from the suburbs.

Brookline's population has grown older at the top and stayed steady in the middle. Census counts
show about 57,100 residents in 2000 and 63,300 in the 2020–24 survey; residents 65 and over rose
from about 7,100 to 10,200, while those 25 to 44, the age of most parents of young children,
stayed near 20,000.

## What this does and does not show

This compares towns, not what would happen within one. Towns full of families also differ in their
lots, commutes and schools, and families who want space choose towns that have it, so the housing
and the families sustain each other. The comparison shows what goes with drawing families. It
cannot say how many families would come if Brookline added family-sized homes, or how quickly.

It does explain why the base case continues Brookline's record. The town's mix of homes changes
slowly, and the homes being built are mostly apartments, which bring fewer children than
houses. [New homes and Brookline's budget](@new-homes) works through what those homes add to
enrollment and cost.

## Data and caveats

- **Births:** Department of Public Health annual birth reports, 2005 to 2023, resident births by
  town. Five-year averages are used because a single year's count in a town of Brookline's size can
  move by 10% on its own.
- **Kindergarten per birth:** DESE's fall 2025 kindergarten count for each town's own district,
  divided by resident births in 2020. It is one year; district counts include METCO students, and
  children in private kindergartens are not counted.
- **Family-sized homes and prices:** each town's assessment records as published by MassGIS. Rooms
  are recorded statewide but bedrooms are not; six or more rooms was checked against Brookline's
  own bedroom counts and matched three or more bedrooms 92.5% of the time. Prices are the last
  recorded sale for each home, from 2023 to 2025, at least $100,000 and half the assessed value.
  Towns' records come from different years (FY2024 to FY2027), and Belmont's include only 45 such
  sales, so its price is less certain.
- **Households, ages, children, bedrooms and housing type:** the Census Bureau's American
  Community Survey, 2020–24 five-year estimates, which carry margins of error of a few percentage
  points for towns this size. Homes include vacant ones. The share in public schools divides
  DESE's fall 2025 kindergarten-to-grade-12 count, which includes METCO students, by the survey's
  children aged 5 to 17, so it is approximate.
- **Single-family values:** the Division of Local Services' average single-family value, FY2010 to
  FY2026. The state leaves Brookline out before FY2022, as it does towns with a residential
  exemption, so Brookline's earlier years follow the change in its own assessment roll, scaled to
  the state's FY2022 figure. (The roll's own average runs about 21% above the state's in every year
  both exist, because the two count somewhat different sets of parcels; only its year-to-year
  change is used.)
- **Correlations** are across twelve towns and describe how closely two measures move together; they
  do not measure cause.
