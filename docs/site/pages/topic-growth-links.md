Most of what a new home costs the Town is a share of services that are already running: a seat
in a classroom, a place on a fire engine's route, a patrol car's time. Whether that share is a
real new cost depends on whether the service has room. This page compares the two ways of
estimating what new homes cost, checks each against what has actually happened in Brookline,
sets out where Brookline stands service by service, and lists the readings that would show the
room running out.

## Two ways to estimate what new homes cost

**The project estimate.** A fiscal impact study for a single building adds up the tax its homes
will pay and subtracts a share of what the Town spends: an average home's share of police, fire
and other departments, and the cost of the students an average home of its kind sends to school.
That is the method the Town's consultants use, and it is the one on
[Growth: when a new home pays for itself](@topic-growth). It assumes the budget moves home by
home, with each project's costs arriving alongside its revenue.

**The town-wide view.** The other way starts from the services. It asks whether the schools,
fire companies and patrols the Town already pays for can take more residents, and at what point
one of them would need another teacher, company or building. Below that point an added home
costs little beyond supplies and the like. At that point, one project can tip a whole new cost.

The two can give very different answers for the same building. Take 100 rental apartments at
about {{fact:homes_value_apts}} of assessed value each, the recent Brookline average. They would
pay about $530,000 a year in property tax. Rounded, in today's dollars:

| | Project estimate | Town-wide view, if open seats and today's staff absorb them |
|---|---|---|
| Students (about 24) | Each with the staff an added student brings, about {{fact:student_cost_about}}: about $400,000 | Each filling an open seat, about $2,500: about $60,000 |
| Town departments | An average home's share, $1,109 before benefits (the consultant RKG's figure for Brookline): about $110,000 | About $660 a home plus benefits, less fire's part, since no fire company is added: about $65,000 |
| **Cost against $530,000 of tax** | **About $510,000: roughly even** | **About $125,000: well ahead** |

**When each is right.** The standard handbook on the method,
[Burchell and Listokin's *Fiscal Impact Guidebook*](https://archive.org/details/fiscalimpactguid024471mbp)
(1978), says the average-cost approach works when a town's services closely match current
demand. Where there is spare capacity, half-empty classrooms or a fire company that can take more
calls, it overstates what growth costs. Where a service is full, it understates it, and the
project that tips the service over gets charged for the whole new facility. The handbook and later
practitioners agree the two approaches meet over the long run and across all of a town's growth
together, but not for one project in one year.

Three further findings from the research shape how this page reads Brookline's record:

- **Projects do not add up one at a time.** Each project is estimated against today's capacity,
  so the cost that the fifth project triggers was caused by all five
  ([Kotval and Mullin, Lincoln Institute](https://lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/kotval-wp06zk2.pdf)).
- **The estimates have hardly ever been checked.** No published study was found comparing a
  project's fiscal estimate with what later happened. One review of the field
  ([Edwards and Huddleston, 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rjpaxx/v76y2010i1p25-41.html))
  found that ready-made models can be wrong by a wide margin, sometimes about whether a project
  comes out ahead or behind.
- **Room is not the same as no cost.** When spending stays flat while a town grows, the cost can
  show up as thinner service for everyone, fewer officers per call or fuller classrooms, rather
  than in the budget ([Paulsen, 2014](https://dpla.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/1021/2017/06/Paulsen-JPL-Effects-Land-Development-Municipal-Finance-as-Published.pdf);
  Ladd's studies of U.S. counties, 1992 and 1994). That is why this page pairs each sign of room
  with a reading that would show it being used up.

The rest of the page tests the town-wide view against Brookline's own record: whether revenue
and costs have actually followed homes, and what the published measures suggest about room in each service.

## Revenue follows construction, about two years later

Every building permit records the applicant's estimate of what the work will cost. Permits on
tax-exempt property, such as Town schools and fire stations, churches and colleges, add no new
growth and are left out here. The timing
runs on the assessment calendar. Massachusetts values every property as of January 1, a
year and a half before the fiscal year that value is taxed in, and new growth is the value added
since the previous January 1. So work permitted during 2023 is mostly built during 2023 and 2024,
valued on January 1, 2025, and taxed as new growth in FY2026, which began in July 2025.

Lined up that way, a calendar year's permits and the new growth they became track each other
closely. From the 2018 to the 2023 permit years, each permitted dollar became

{{fact:gl_ng_per_permit}} in the middle year. That alignment fits better than any other tested,
including lining permits up by fiscal year (a correlation of {{fact:gl_permit_r}} over
{{fact:gl_permit_n}} years, against well under half that by fiscal year).

### What new homes add, and what renovation adds

New homes are the smaller part of residential new growth. From FY2018 to FY2025, renovation,
including houses rebuilt as houses, added about $970 million of it, against about $650 million
from the buildings that added homes. And the total has held steady whatever the pace of building:
residential new growth has stayed between 0.74% and 1.02% of the levy limit every year since
2009, whether about 40 or about 180 homes a year were being permitted. When fewer homes are built,
renovation fills in ([Growth: renovation and what faster growth is worth](@topic-growth-value)).

What a new home adds depends on its kind. Brookline's permits since 2008 were read one by one and
each project followed onto the assessment rolls. Each net new home in an apartment building
permitted from 2015 to 2022 added about {{fact:homes_value_apts}} of assessed value, and each in
a condominium or small multifamily building about {{fact:homes_value_condos}}. The homes the
forecast adds, the two in their recent proportions, average about {{fact:homes_value}}, about
$6,000 a year in tax at the FY2026 rate. A house that adds a home carries the value of the whole
new building, several million dollars, which is why houses look so different in a per-home
estimate ([Counting Brookline's homes](@topic-homes)).

### What others have found about children in new homes

Studies elsewhere point the same way, with some cautions.

- **Massachusetts, district by district.** The Metropolitan Area Planning Council compared new
  housing with public school enrollment across Massachusetts districts, first for 2010 to 2016
  ([2017](https://www.mapc.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/MAPC_HousingEnrollment_Final.pdf)) and
  again for 2010 to 2020 using the Census
  ([2024 update](https://www.mapc.org/learn/research-analysis/enrollment/)). Across 231 districts
  it found no significant link: in its own region housing grew 7.9% over the decade while
  enrollment fell 0.3%. The test on this page finds the same.
- **Massachusetts, building by building.** A UMass study for MassBenchmarks
  ([Rapoza and Goodman, 2019](https://donahue.umass.edu/documents/MB_072919_low-re.pdf)) asked
  school districts how many students actually lived in 42 new developments with 6,076 homes. Taken
  together they paid their towns $7.7 million a year more than they cost, about $1,273 a home.
  But 12 of the 42 cost their town more than they paid, most often where schools were already
  full or students needed costly special education.
- **Brookline itself.** A 2007 UMass Donahue Institute study for the Citizens' Housing and Planning
  Association ([report](https://donahue.umass.edu/documents/UMDI_FiscalImpact.pdf)) counted the
  students in eight mixed-income developments, one of them Kendall Crescent in Brookline: 35
  homes, 5 public school students. None of the eight had a measurable effect on the cost of public
  services, and school costs were rising in towns with falling enrollment as well as rising.
- **New Hampshire.** A 2024 study for New Hampshire Housing
  ([From Homes to Classrooms](https://www.nhhfa.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/From_Homes_To_Classrooms_2024.pdf))
  matched students to their home addresses in four towns. Homes built from 2014 to 2023 had 0.16
  public school students each, against 0.29 for all housing statewide; new single-family homes had
  0.40 and new apartments and condominiums 0.06. The two towns whose new housing was mostly
  single-family homes came out behind.
- **New Jersey.** A Rutgers study of about 40,000 rental apartments
  ([2018](https://highlandsnj.gov/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/the-school-age-children-study-rcre-july-2018.pdf))
  found 9.8 school-age children per 100 market-rate apartments built after 2000, against 25.9 in
  older ones, and about 64 per 100 affordable apartments of either age.
- **Newton.** The demographer who studies Brookline's enrollment reached the same conclusion for
  Newton in 2024 ([study](https://figcitynews.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/NPS-MA-Demographic-Study-Report-McKibben240214-1.pdf)):
  growth in households whose children have grown, and the pace of existing home sales, drive
  enrollment more than new construction.

The cautions run the same way in every study. New apartments and condominiums, especially smaller
ones, bring few children; family-sized homes, affordable homes and single-family subdivisions
bring more, sometimes as many as existing homes or more; and a town whose schools are full feels
each added student more than one with open seats. The Massachusetts building-by-building study
is the clearest case: where its developments cost their towns more than they paid, full schools
were among the most common reasons.

### Schools: open seats now, and more coming

The schools were rebuilt and expanded for the enrollment peak of FY2019. Since then enrollment has
fallen. On October 1 kindergarten through grade 8 had 4,436 students in 236 classes, with about 1,100
open seats under the School Committee's own class-size guidelines. No grade at any school is over
its guideline. The guideline is a ceiling, though. In 2017 the School Department presented an
average class of 24 as the outcome to avoid, and this fall's classes average 19. With every class
kept three students below its guideline, about 440 seats are open. The fullest schools are Pierce
(87%), which moves into its larger new building in fall 2028, and Ruffin Ridley (84%); the
emptiest is Hayes (75%). The high school, expanded in 2022, has about 535 seats more than its
2,165 students. In the base case enrollment falls further, to
{{fact:enroll_fy2040_about}} by FY2040, because fewer children are being born to Brookline
residents. At the forecast's 0.24 students a home, the seats opening by FY2030 would take
{{fact:seats_homes_fy2030}} homes beyond the recent pace to fill if staff were held at the FY2027
level; with staff falling half as fast as enrollment, as in the base case, about half as many
([Schools: staffing, costs and open seats](@topic-schools#filling-an-open-seat)).

Two limits. Seats sit in particular schools and grades, and a large building's children all go to
one school's district, so a project near Ruffin Ridley or Pierce uses scarcer room than one near
Lincoln. And the open seats could instead be cut, by holding fewer teachers as enrollment falls,
as the base case partly does;
then students from new homes mean fewer positions cut, and their cost is the saving given up,
which is what the project estimate measures.

### Fire: companies set by coverage more than by workload

The Fire Department runs five engine companies and two ladder companies from five stations, each
staffed with at least four firefighters around the clock, 29 on duty each day. It responds to
more than 95% of calls in under four minutes. In FY2025 it made about 10,500 responses, or about
1,500 for each company if each call took one. Fire consultants treat about 3,000 runs a year as
the mark of a busy company. Calls bring out more than one company, and the busiest companies,
in Brookline Village and Coolidge Corner, run well above the average, so the true figures are
higher. Even so, by these measures the average company is well below the usual marks of a busy
one, though the busiest may be closer than the average shows.

That is not what decides when a company is added. The standards fire departments are measured
against set targets for how quickly the first engine and the full response arrive, which depend
on distance, not on the number of homes. The one company the Department has costed, a ladder for
South Brookline, is a question of coverage: how quickly a ladder truck reaches the south of
town. The Fire Chief would place it at Station 4 on Route 9, which is being rebuilt to house two
companies, so the Town could add it in any year, with or without new homes. The forecast's own
step, a company once the town reaches {{fact:fire_company_homes}} homes, is the point at which
Brookline's firefighters per home would fall to the median of 21 nearby cities and towns. It is
a placeholder for when growth alone might call for one, not a measure of capacity, and at the
recent pace of building it falls after FY2040.

The pressure on fire today comes from elsewhere. Medical calls rose about 7% a year from FY2021
to FY2025, while homes grew about half a percent a year, most likely because Brookline's population is
growing older: residents 65 and over grew 36% from 2010 to 2020. Overtime has run over budget
every year since FY2021, by $0.65 million to $2.1 million, which the Town attributes to leave
rules in the FY2018 contract and to injured leave up about 150%. Those are costs of the people
already here, not of new homes, but they are what would make the Department ask for more
firefighters first.

### Police: fewer calls, short of its own staffing

Police calls fell from about 75,600 in FY2013 to about 53,000 to 56,000 in FY2024 and FY2025,
while homes were added. Part of the fall is a change in how calls were counted, and most of the
rest is less activity started by officers themselves, such as traffic stops, while the department
ran 14 to 19 officers below its authorized strength; 911 calls rose. So the counts say nothing
about new homes adding police work, and they should not be read as falling need either. The
budget funds 128 sworn officers; the department's mid-2026 report puts it 18 below its authorized
strength. The likelier police cost is filling those positions, a decision about service levels,
not about growth. The forecast keeps a small police cost for each new home,
{{fact:ps_share_police_per_home}} a year, rather than a step.

Comparisons of officers per resident are common but a poor test of room. They leave out the
commuters, hospital staff, students and shoppers Brookline serves who do not live here, and they
measure a town's size rather than how stretched its officers are. Brookline's officers per home
are close to the median of nearby towns; that says it is staffed like its peers, not that it has
room or lacks it. The measures that would show room, the share of patrol officers' time spent
answering calls and how long priority calls wait, are not published.

### Other Town departments

The other Town departments, from public works and libraries to general government and human
services, added about 40 positions from FY2021 to FY2025, after a decade of holding steady while
homes were added. No published measure says
whether they have room for more residents. The forecast charges about $660 a year for each new

departments, which is the cautious way to treat a service whose room cannot be measured.

### What these measures cannot see

Every figure above comes from budgets, reports and public data. None comes from the people who run
the schools, the Fire Department or the Police Department, and none of them has been asked for
this page whether they have room. They may well feel stretched now. Services usually strain before
any measure of capacity says they are full, and in ways these figures miss:

- **People before positions.** Overtime, injured and sick leave, vacancies and turnover wear on a
  department long before its call volume looks high. The Fire Department's overtime and leave and
  the Police Department's vacancies are signs of strain of that kind, whatever their cause.
- **Particular places and hours.** A town-wide average hides the busiest company, the busiest
  shift and the neighborhood farthest from a station. An open seat in one school does not help a
  full grade in another.
- **Space other than classrooms.** Rooms for special education, English learners and specialists,
  cafeterias and gyms can fill before classes reach their size guideline.
- **What each student or call needs.** A growing share of students with special needs, or of
  calls that are medical, changes what each seat or call takes.
- **Thinner service rather than more cost.** Brookline added homes through the 2010s with flat
  police and fire staffing. That could mean the Town had room, or that service thinned as the same
  staff covered more residents. The Fire Department's response times, under four minutes for more
  than 95% of calls, suggest fire service has not thinned town-wide. For police no published
  reading could tell.

So where each service's limit lies is not known, and this page does not claim to know it. The
published readings can show which way things are moving. The rest is a question for the people
who run these services.

### What to ask the people who run these services

The first question is the simplest: where do they feel stretched now, and what would they expect
to need first if the town grew faster? The reading above would also be firmer with five things
their departments hold but do not publish:

1. **Fire runs by company and response times by station**, at the 90th percentile, for
   2015 to 2025. These would show which companies are busiest and whether any part of town is losing
   ground.
2. **The 2019 firefighters' union analysis of ladder coverage in South Brookline**, cited by the
   Advisory Committee in 2022 but not posted.
3. **Police patrol staffing**: the minimum number of officers on each shift, the number of patrol
   sectors, and the share of patrol time spent on calls, by hour.
4. **Police response times** to priority calls.
5. **School seats by grade at each school**, as the School Department projects them each spring,
   published rather than shared on request.

## What to watch

The readings below would show, years ahead in most cases, which way things are moving: whether
there is room for new homes, and whether something needs to expand. Where the true thresholds lie
is not known. Those marked as this page's own are judgments, not standards, and the people who run
each service may well set them differently.

**Whether there is room for new homes**

| Reading | Where it is published | What would signal less room |
|---|---|---|
| Open seats by school and grade | School Department projections each spring; state enrollment each October | A school above about 90% of its seats at guideline (this page's mark), or a grade at its guideline |
| Kindergarten against births five years earlier | State birth reports; October enrollment | Kindergarten per birth rising above its 2006 to 2025 range, as families move in |
| Enrollment per Brookline birth | October enrollment | Rising, as it did in the 2010s, when families moved into existing homes |
| Homes permitted | The Town's building permits | A pace well above {{fact:homes_pace}} net new homes a year, or a large family-sized project in one school's district |

**Whether something needs to expand**

| Reading | Where it is published | What would signal a need |
|---|---|---|
| Fire response times | Fire Department figures in each Financial Plan | Fewer than 90% of calls reached in four minutes, or the full response in eight (the national standards' measure) |

| Fire overtime | Each Financial Plan; Reserve Fund transfers | Overtime over budget year after year, a sign of staffing strain rather than of homes |

So the budget can be seen coming, but not through any single project. Revenue from construction
is visible about two years out. Enrollment is visible three to four years out from births and a year
out from the grade counts, and it depends on the whole town's births and turnover. Town staffing
changes in steps that can be seen approaching as the town's total grows.

## What this means for a single project

A project estimate is a fair description of one building's average share. It is a poor guide to
what the budget will do where services have room. Revenue from a project arrives on a schedule
that can be seen about two years ahead. Its costs do not arrive with it: police and fire have not
grown with homes, and school enrollment has moved with births and with turnover in the existing
housing far more than with the newest buildings. The useful questions about a project are
town-wide ones:

- **Is it part of the pace the forecast already counts?** The forecast assumes
  {{fact:homes_pace}} net new homes a year, mostly apartments and condominiums.
- **Does it use scarce room?** A family-sized building in the Ruffin Ridley or Pierce district
  uses fuller schools than one elsewhere.
- **Does it bring a step closer?** With everything else being built, does it bring the town
  nearer a fire company or a full grade?

Where the answers are yes, a project estimate's costs become real, and the project that tips a
step can carry a large one. Where they are no, the estimate likely overstates the cost, though
how close any service is to a step is not something the published figures can settle
([Navigating the next few years](@next-few-years)).

## Data and caveats

- **The comparison of the two estimates.** The 100-apartment example uses the recent value per
  apartment home, the FY2026 residential tax rate (1.024%), 0.244 students a home, the forecast's
  {{fact:student_cost}} for an added student with staff and $2,497 for one filling an open seat, RKG
  Associates' $1,109 a home for Town departments, before benefits, and the forecast's $660 a
  home plus benefits, less fire's share. The figures mix FY2025 to FY2027 dollars and are rounded; the
  [breakeven test](@topic-growth) works the same comparison carefully.
- **Research on the method.** Burchell and Listokin's guidebook, Paulsen (2014) and Kotval and
  Mullin were read in full; Edwards and Huddleston (2010) and Ladd (1992, 1994) only in their
  published summaries.
- **Fire.** Companies, staffing, overtime and the cost of a ladder company from the FY2027
  Financial Plan and the Town's FY2024-25 Fire Department overtime and leave memo (revised March
  2025). Responses from the Financial Plans' workload tables, with FY2019 to FY2023 filled from
  the Town's online budget pages. The 3,000-runs mark comes from fire consultants' reports cited
  in a 2018 U.S. Fire Administration paper; no standard sets a workload at which a company must be
  added. Runs per company assume one company per call, which understates them.
- **Police.** Calls from the Financial Plans and the department's annual and mid-year reports,
  which count calls two different ways over the years; staffing from the FY2027 budget and the
  2026 mid-year report.
- **Schools.** Open seats from the School Department's FY2027 projections shared with the
  Advisory Committee, against the School Committee's class-size guidelines (22 in kindergarten to
  grade 2, 25 in grades 3 to 8, lower at Pierce and Driscoll); the high school figure is the
  enrollment the expanded building was designed for.
- **Home sales and kindergarten.** The School Department's demographic study (2023) expects sales
  of existing homes, more than new construction, to drive enrollment as households whose children
  have grown sell to younger ones. Years with more single-family sales have had somewhat more
  kindergartners per birth (a correlation of {{fact:gl_sales_r}} over {{fact:gl_sales_n}} years,
  leaving out fall 2020), but sixteen years is too few to call this proven, condominiums with
  three or more bedrooms show no such pattern, and in the older grades the students joining or
  leaving each year have not risen with sales (correlations of {{fact:gl_movers_r0}} and
  {{fact:gl_movers_r1}}). Sales come from the Town's assessment rolls, FY2009 to FY2026, $100,000
  or more; one roll (FY2022) could not be read.
- **Business equipment in new growth.** The state's certified new growth includes business
  equipment (personal property), about an eighth to a sixth of it in FY2022 to FY2026 by the Board
  of Assessors' figures, which is not built under building permits. The chart and the cents per
  permitted dollar include it; the check on FY2027 and FY2028 sets it aside and compares real
  estate with real estate.
- **Permits and new growth.** Town Building Department permit records, calendar 2018 to 2025
  (the current system, which starts in mid-2017; the earlier one has no cost field), counted once
  per permit. The cost is the applicant's own estimate. New growth: Massachusetts Division of
  Local Services, certified new growth valuation by fiscal year. Six permit years have certified
  new growth so far, so the fit rests on six points; windows of 12, 24 and 36 months ending at
  different dates were compared, and this one fit best. The estimated cost is summed over every
  permit type, so a renovation's building permit and its plumbing, electrical and mechanical
  permits all count; the ratio is measured on that same total. Counting building permits alone
  fit no better.
- **Timing, property by property.** Assessed value of every parcel FY2009 to FY2026, from the
  Town's assessment rolls, against each parcel's permits, matched by street address. A job's
  added value is its rise above the median growth of parcels with no permit, through the FY2026
  roll (January 1, 2025); a job counts when that rise exceeds 10% of the value before the permit.
  Newer permits: one building permit of $250,000 or more on the parcel. Older permits (no cost
  recorded): renovations, additions and new buildings by the permit's description, with no other
  building permit on the parcel from two years before to four years after.
- **Homes on the assessment rolls.** Units per parcel, FY2017 to FY2025 rolls. Removed: hotels,
  a condominium parking lot, a private school, and any change of 15 or more units on a parcel
  whose value did not move with it. Rolls before FY2017 have no unit field, and the FY2026 roll
  counts units differently, so the series stops at FY2025.
- **Staffing and homes.** Budgeted positions from the Town's Financial Plans; homes from the 2010
  and 2020 Censuses, carried between them at the recent pace, so the homes line is smooth by
  construction.
- **Enrollment.** Massachusetts Department of Elementary and Secondary Education, enrollment by
  grade, October of each school year; resident births from the state's annual birth reports.
  New units authorized: Census Bureau Building Permits Survey, annual place files, which include
  the Bureau's estimates for months not reported. The survey counts units in new buildings only.
- **Children per added home.** Census 2010 and 2020 redistricting counts: residents under 18 and
  housing units, for the {{fact:gl_towns_n}} cities and towns with at least 2,000 homes, each town
  one observation, the growth in children set against the growth in homes. A comparison between
  towns over one decade, not an experiment.
- **Method and code.** The code, every series behind this page, and research notes on the fire
  and police sources, page by page, are in the [project's public files on
  GitHub](https://github.com/benjammin-code/brookline-budget-2040).
