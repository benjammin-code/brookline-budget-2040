This page sets out how the schools reached their current size, how their staffing compares, and
what an added student costs. How many students there will be is on
[Enrollment: three forecasts compared](@topic-enrollment), and why Brookline draws fewer young
families than its suburban neighbors is on [Why families settle elsewhere](@topic-families). The
[new homes page](@new-homes) applies all of it to new housing school by school.

## Enrollment, open seats and new housing

A common worry is that new housing fills the schools with children who cost more to educate than
their homes pay in tax. The [breakeven test](@topic-growth) answers that one building at a time.
The town-wide answer turns mostly on something else: Brookline is having far fewer children.
Brookline residents had about 690 babies a year in 2008–13 and {{fact:ind_births}} in
{{fact:ind_births_year}}, and each birth year becomes a kindergarten class five years later, so
the effect on the schools is still arriving.

Enrollment depends on births and on families moving in and out. One number summarizes the second: enrollment divided by the children born to
Brookline residents in the thirteen years that feed kindergarten through grade 12. It rises when
families with children move in or stay, whether into new homes or, far more often, into the
29,000 homes already here, and falls when they leave.

| | FY2005 | FY2019 | FY2021 | FY2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enrollment | 5,984 | 7,855 | 6,891 | 7,023 |
| Children born to Brookline residents in the 13 years feeding K–12 | 8,074 | 8,735 | 8,749 | 8,408 |
| Enrollment per Brookline birth | 0.74 | 0.90 | 0.79 | 0.84 |

**How the 2010s rise happened.** From FY2005 to FY2019 enrollment rose 31%, from 5,984 to 7,855.
The pool of Brookline-born children feeding the schools grew only 8% over the same years, while
enrollment per birth rose from 0.74 to 0.90. Roughly a quarter of the rise came from more births
and three-quarters from families moving in, staying, and choosing the public schools, mostly in
homes that already existed. The schools felt it. A September 2019 School Department staff
planning draft describes "historic enrollment increases, overcrowding, and substandard spaces,"
refers to planning work since 2009 to respond, names Pierce, Driscoll, Baker and Runkle as
overcrowded, and plans to move classrooms out of leased space. Voters approved debt exclusions to
rebuild or expand Devotion (now Ruffin Ridley, 2015), the High School (2018), Driscoll (2019) and
Pierce (2023). Those buildings were sized for the peak. Enrollment fell to 6,891 in FY2021, during
COVID, and has held near 7,000 since, which is why the FY2027 kindergarten-to-grade-8 classes have
about 945 empty seats (the [new homes page](@new-homes) sets them out school by school).

{{chart:enroll-seats}}

**So far, families moving in have held enrollment level.** From FY2021 to FY2025 the pool of
Brookline-born children fell 4%, yet enrollment stayed near 7,000. Had enrollment per birth
stayed at its FY2021 level, FY2025 would have had about 6,620 students, roughly 400 fewer.
Families moving into Brookline made up the difference.

**From here, smaller birth years bring enrollment down.** In the base case it falls from 6,889
this fall to {{fact:enroll_fy2030}} in FY2030 and {{fact:enroll_fy2040_about}} by FY2040, even
with families continuing to move in at about the recent rate. The School Department's own forecast
instead expects a rebound, mainly because it assumes more births than have been counted.
[Enrollment: three forecasts compared](@topic-enrollment) sets the forecasts side by side, and
[Why families settle elsewhere](@topic-families) compares Brookline with the suburbs that draw more
young families.

**Where new housing fits.** New homes bring their students through the same in-migration steps
as any family buying or renting an existing home. At the recent pace of {{fact:homes_pace}} homes a
year, plus Chestnut Hill, {{fact:base_homes_added_fy40}} homes by FY2040 would bring about
{{fact:base_students_low}} to {{fact:base_students_high}} students, using
0.17 (the middle of ten recent Brookline buildings) to 0.24 (the forecast's average). That is
real, and it is smaller than the decline from births. Those students arrive in a system with
fewer students than it had: the district served about 7,850 students in FY2019 and is projected
to serve {{fact:enroll_fy2040_about}} in FY2040 under NESDEC (updated for actual births), which already includes homes built at the recent
pace.

**What that means for cost.** It depends on what the schools do as enrollment falls. If staffing
stays where it is, as the School Department's own plan assumes, students from new homes fill
seats that would otherwise sit empty and add little cost beyond the forecast. If staffing shrinks
with enrollment, as the base case assumes at half the pace of enrollment (the FY2019 to FY2025
pattern), students from new homes mean fewer positions cut, and their cost is the saving forgone, which is what the per-home
[breakeven test](@topic-growth) measures. Either way, the town-wide question is not whether new
housing will crowd the schools. It is how quickly enrollment falls, and whether staffing follows
it. [Indicators to watch](@topic-enrollment#indicators-to-watch) tracks both each fall.

Two cautions. Births for 2025 and later are not yet counted (2024's 443 comes from the Town's annual
report); another low year like 2023 would deepen the decline. And the number of children a building brings varies widely with its bedrooms
and tenure (0.03 to 0.56 in the ten recent buildings), so the per-home test still matters for any
one project.

## Two enrollment forecasts that disagree

Two demographers produced forecasts for the district in 2023, and they point in opposite
directions ([Enrollment: three forecasts compared](@topic-enrollment) sets them side by side with the base case). [[NESDEC]] projects enrollment falling to about 6,130 by 2033–34.
[[Cropper-McKibben]], the forecast the School Department plans with, projects a low in 2027–28
and a rise to about 7,440. Both have been close so far.

| School year | Actual | NESDEC | Cropper-McKibben |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2024–25 | 7,023 | 7,013 (−0.1%) | 6,980 (−0.6%) |
| 2025–26 | 6,948 | 6,880 (−1.0%) | 6,929 (−0.3%) |
| 2026–27 | 6,889 (Sept.) | 6,774 (−1.7%) | 6,917 (+0.4%) |
| 2033–34 | | **6,127** | **7,444** |

Cropper-McKibben has tracked the last two years slightly better. The base case still uses
NESDEC because the population data points the same way: births to Brookline residents fell from
about 690 a year (2008–13) to about 490 (2020–22), and UMass Donahue Institute projections have
Brookline's 5–14 age group falling about 28% from 2020 to 2035. With school staff falling half as
fast as enrollment as it falls and rising with it as it rises, as in the base case, the choice
between the two moves FY2040 by {{fact:fy40_enroll_cm_abs}}.

## How Brookline's staffing compares

{{chart:staff-enroll}}

Against 17 peer K–12 districts, Brookline had 5.6 students per staff member in FY2025, the 7th
most staffed of 18 (peer median 5.9), and the same rank ten years earlier. Its needs rank 4th: 41%
high-needs students (median 30%) and 11% English learners (median 4%). Peers added staff faster
than enrollment too: over ten years Brookline's staff grew 10% while enrollment fell 6.5%; the
peer median was +17% staff and −4% enrollment. Brookline serves most students with disabilities
in-district, so its out-of-district tuition is low (5% of spending, 16th of 18). What stands out
is pay: Brookline's average teacher salary ($132,000 in FY2025) is the highest of the 17 districts
with data, against a peer median of about $112,000.

**For the forecast.** Staffing is somewhat above peers and so are needs, so there is no strong
case for treating it as an outlier to be trimmed. School pay growth is the larger open question,
and it is being bargained now. [How Brookline got here](@spending) shows how the
students-per-staff ratio moved from FY2011 to FY2025.

## How the cost of an added student is calculated

The forecast treats two-thirds of the school budget as moving with enrollment and one-third as
fixed (buildings, administration, leadership), the split RKG set with Town staff. It staffs the
enrollment-linked share at the FY2027 ratio of 5.55 students per staff member (6,889 students,
about 1,242 staff). An added student therefore brings 0.67 ÷ 5.55 = 0.121 positions, or one
position per 8.3 students.

| Component | FY2027 dollars |
|---|---|
| Staff: 0.121 positions × $100,109 (FY2027 school salary budget per staff member) | $12,092 |
| Non-personnel: 17% of the budget per pupil ($3,697) × 0.67 | $2,477 |
| Health insurance: 0.121 positions × active-staff health cost per FTE | $1,839 |
| Medicare and other payroll-linked benefits | $429 |
| **Cost of one added student** | **{{fact:student_cost}}** |

That is 77% of the $21,745 average school budget per pupil in FY2027, and close to RKG's $15,572
(FY2025), which uses the same two-thirds split. It rises with pay and premiums: about $22,500 in
FY2034 and $28,900 in FY2040 in nominal dollars. Teacher pensions are paid by the state and aren't
included. In practice the cost is lumpy: with K–8 classes averaging 17 to 21 against guidelines
of 22 to 25, a child joining a grade with room costs little, while one that forces a new section
costs a teacher. {{fact:student_cost}} is the long-run average.

## Filling an open seat

The cost above assumes each added student brings added staff. That is the right assumption for a
growing school system, but Brookline's is shrinking: enrollment is projected to fall from 6,889 in
FY2027 to {{fact:enroll_fy2040_about}} by FY2040, while the base case lets school staff fall only half as
fast, as they did from FY2019 to FY2025 (the School Department's own plan keeps staffing at the
FY2027 level). The FY2027 kindergarten-to-grade-8 classes already have about 945 open seats under
the School Committee's class-size guidelines, and more open as enrollment falls faster than staff.
A new home's children can fill seats the Town is already paying for, and the
added cost per student is about $2,477 (supplies, transport and the other costs that move with
each student) rather than {{fact:student_cost}}. On that basis even a $434,000 rental apartment nets about $3,000 a
year. With staff held at the FY2027 level, the seats that open as enrollment falls would take
{{fact:seats_homes_fy2030}} homes beyond the recent pace by FY2030 and {{fact:seats_homes_fy2040}} by FY2040;
with staff falling half as fast, as in the base case, about half as many. The open seats could instead be cut to save money, so
the two uses compete. The [new homes page](@new-homes) sets this out school by school, and the
Forecast Explorer's *Students from added homes* setting runs it through the forecast.

**The $2,477 is probably high.** The base case builds it from an estimate that 17% of the school
budget is non-personnel spending: 17% of $149.8 million is about $25.5 million, the two-thirds of
that which moves with enrollment is $17.1 million, and divided by 6,889 students that is $2,477.
The 17% is this project's own estimate, derived from Town-wide spending by object class. The
Schools' own FY2026 year-end report shows less: $125.0 million of salaries and $17.8 million of
non-salary spending, so non-salary is 12.5% of the budget. On that share the same calculation
gives about **$1,800** a student. Most of the $17.8 million does not grow when one more child
joins a class that is already running:

| FY2026 non-salary budget line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Transportation (largely special education) | $5.2M |
| Out-of-district special education tuition | $3.0M |
| Professional and technical services | $2.9M |
| General supplies | $1.7M |
| Claims and settlements | $1.4M |
| Online books, subscriptions and tools | $1.3M |
| Leased and purchased computers | $0.9M |
| Textbooks and print materials | $0.6M |
| Legal services | $0.4M |
| Everything else | $0.3M |
| **Total** | **$17.8M** |

The lines that really scale with each seat (supplies, textbooks, online tools, computers, field
trips and instructional equipment) come to about $4.6 million, roughly **$660 a student**.
Transportation and out-of-district tuition follow particular students, mostly those with special
education needs, rather than seats. So the cost of a student filling an open seat is somewhere
between about $660 and the base case's $2,477, and probably nearer the lower end. The base case
keeps $2,477, which makes its open-seat results conservative; a lower figure would only widen the
gap between the two cases. (Source: Public Schools of Brookline, FY26 General Fund Financial
Report, Line Item, 4th Quarter, 14 August 2026, budget column.)
