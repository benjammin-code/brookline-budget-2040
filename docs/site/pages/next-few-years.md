## Where things stand

If costs and revenue keep behaving the way they have since 2011, and counting the override voters
approved in May 2026, revenue covers spending every year to 2040, with room of
{{fact:room_fy2028}} in FY2028, {{fact:room_2030s_low}} to {{fact:room_2030s_high}} a year through the 2030s and
{{fact:room_fy2040}} in FY2040. [The base case](@base-case) explains how.

The next few years are the thinnest, and the first risk to them is already visible. Building
permits point to FY2028 new growth well below the trend (below). If it comes in there, FY2028
would have {{fact:fy2028_after_permits}} instead of {{fact:room_fy2028}} of room.

That room depends on assumptions that are themselves decisions. Pay grows at the Town's
{{fact:pay_town}} bargaining target, with schools ({{fact:pay_school}}) and Public Safety
({{fact:pay_ps}}) above it, for average pay growth across all staff of {{fact:pay_avg}} a year.
School staff fall about half as fast as enrollment as it declines, as they did from FY2019 to
FY2025. Health premiums keep rising about 12% a year through FY2029, then settle at 8% a
year. The pension money freed in FY2031 goes to paying down the retiree health liability, and
once that is paid off it is set aside for a decision rather than counted as room. Any one
of these going the other way moves FY2040 by more than the whole room. Other assumptions follow
the Town's own plan or policies, on the cautious side: state aid grows 2.5% a year, and free cash
left after capital and reserves goes to capital and one-time uses rather than the operating
budget.

## The decisions ahead

**Pay contracts.** If pay for all staff grows 1 point a year faster, {{fact:pay_avg_up1}} on
average instead of {{fact:pay_avg}}, it costs
{{fact:fy40_pay_up1_abs}} a year by FY2040, and the room would
fall a little short in FY2028 and run out for good from {{fact:runs_out_pay_up1}}. The teachers' contracts expired in August 2026 and are being
negotiated now; other Town contracts follow on their own cycles.

**Health plan costs.** Premiums rising 10% a year instead of 8% from FY2030 cost
{{fact:fy40_premiums_high_abs}} a year by FY2040. Brookline does not set the rate, but it does set
its share of the premium and the plan design, in negotiation with its unions.

**School staffing as enrollment changes.** The district's consultant, NESDEC, forecasts falling
enrollment; updated for the births since counted, that is {{fact:enroll_fy2040_about}} students by
2040. The School Department's own forecast has it rebounding. The base case has school staff
falling half as fast as enrollment, about ten positions a year, which retirements and departures
would likely cover. Holding school staff at the FY2027 level instead, as the School Department's
FY2027 to FY2029 plan does, keeps that money in classrooms that have fewer students: it costs
{{fact:fy40_staff_keep_abs}} a year by FY2040, and the room would
fall a little short in FY2028 and run out for good from {{fact:runs_out_staff_keep}}. If enrollment-linked positions follow enrollment down fully, FY2040
improves by {{fact:fy40_staff_follow_abs}} more.

**The pension money freed after FY2030.** When the pension system reaches full funding, its
catch-up payments end. The Town's plan moves that money to the retiree health liability from
FY2031; counting it as room instead would add {{fact:fy40_pension_kept_abs}} a year to the room
by FY2040. The cost is that the retiree health liability, about {{fact:opeb_net_fy2027}} in
FY2027, would keep rising, as it does now, because the benefit builds faster than the Town
currently funds it. With the money moved, $43.0 million a year, the liability is paid off in
{{fact:opeb_paid_off}} on the actuary's own trend for retiree health costs, and the money is free
again, {{fact:opeb_freed_fy2040}} a year by FY2040. The base case sets that money aside for a decision then and doesn't count it as room;
counted, the room in FY2040 would be {{fact:room_fy2040_with_freed}}. If retirees' costs grew as
fast as the base case's 8% premiums, the liability would not be paid off before 2040. Retirees'
health care would still be paid each year, but more of what today's employees are earning toward
it would be left to later budgets. Rating agencies weigh this, though Moody's lists it behind
reserves and school debt among Brookline's concerns.

**Police and fire staffing.** Fire and medical calls have grown about 3% a year since FY2013,
much faster than housing, with medical emergencies much of the rise. A fire company of 20
firefighters comes due once the town reaches about {{fact:fire_company_homes}} homes; at the
recent pace of building that is after FY2040, so the base case adds none. If the calls keep
rising, the same money could pay for emergency medical response sooner. Police calls have fallen since FY2016,
so the base case holds police at the FY2027 budget of 128 officers, with police cost growing a
little with each new home. Adding officers as homes are added, to keep pace with nearby cities
and towns, would mean {{fact:police_added_fy2040}} more officers by FY2040 and cost
{{fact:fy40_police_step_abs}} a year by then. Returning to earlier staffing, police to 136
officers as in FY2018 to FY2020 and fire to 168 firefighters as in FY2004, would add 16 positions
from today's budget and cost {{fact:fy40_ps_restore_abs}} a year by FY2040. The base case already
counts the increases agreed: new fire steps from FY2028, and four police positions refilled with
override money in FY2028 and FY2029.

**Zoning, one parcel at a time.** A project-by-project estimate charges each new home its own
share of police, firefighters and teachers. The Town's budget has not moved that way: costs arrive
in steps when town-wide growth crosses a threshold, or barely move when there is spare capacity.
[New homes and Brookline's budget](@new-homes) shows the difference. For a single project, two
other questions say more:

- **Is it part of the growth the forecast already counts?** The base case assumes {{fact:homes_pace}} new
  homes a year, and that pace is made up of individual projects being approved. A project within
  it is already in the forecast's revenue and costs. Without it and others like it, the town falls
  short of that pace and the room shrinks. A project that takes the town above that pace adds to
  the room, as the growth section below shows.
- **Does it bring the town closer to a cost step?** Children from new homes can fill empty
  classroom seats while there are seats to fill. And every added home brings the town closer to
  the {{fact:fire_company_homes}} homes at which a fire company comes due.

**Zoning by area.** The comprehensive planning process now under way is identifying areas where
zoning could allow more housing and commercial space. That is where the growth lever is mostly
decided.

**Capital.** The Select Board's policy puts 6.6% of the prior year's net revenue into capital.
The Town held it at 6% for FY2026 and FY2027 for operating relief and plans to return to it in
FY2028, and the base case does the same. Holding capital at about 6% instead would leave
{{fact:fy40_capital_table_abs}} a year more room by FY2040, with less spent on repairs and
equipment. Large school
buildings are voted separately, as debt exclusions, and appear on the tax bill rather than in
this budget.

## Growth: what the base case already assumes, and what more is worth

The base case is not a no-growth case. It assumes new growth of {{fact:trend_growth_rate}} of the
levy a year, Brookline's recent rate, which includes {{fact:homes_pace}} new homes a year, the
pace of the building permits issued from 2016 to 2025, up from {{fact:homes_pace_2008_15}} a year
from 2008 to 2015. Keeping it takes steady zoning, permitting and approval work, and it arrives
unevenly: {{fact:homes_permits_2022}} homes were permitted in 2022 and {{fact:homes_permits_2025}} in
2025. Building fewer would shrink the room: with renovation and commercial growth at their pace,
half as many new homes would leave {{fact:fy40_homes_half_abs}} less room a year by FY2040, and
none {{fact:fy40_homes_none_abs}} less. Those figures take away the homes' tax but leave the
enrollment forecast as it is, so they show the most the loss could be; net of their students and
services, the homes the forecast adds bring in {{fact:homes_net_fy2040}} a year more than they
cost by FY2040. The Explorer's New homes built setting shows both.

Growing faster is worth more, and its kind matters. Reaching the pace of the fastest-growing fifth of Massachusetts towns
through commercial space would add {{fact:fy40_peer_commercial_abs}} a year by FY2040; through
new homes alone, {{fact:fy40_peer_housing_abs}}, because new homes bring students and services as
well as tax. If enrollment declines as forecast, the schools' empty seats could take the children from
{{fact:seats_homes_fy2030}} more homes by 2030 and {{fact:seats_homes_fy2040}} more by 2040.

The budget is not the only limit. The Planning Department has flagged two physical ones: only
some parts of town can be served by the electric grid for large buildings, and some
neighborhoods already have far less open space per person than others. Traffic and access to
transit bear on where added homes work well. None of these is in the forecast, and each bears on
where, and how much, to rezone.

## What to watch, and what each reading tells you

A few numbers, read once a year, show whether the forecast is holding and which decision is
coming due.

**The first reading is already in, and it is below the base case.** Building permits show new
growth about two years ahead ([New homes and the budget](@topic-growth-links)). Permits
on taxable property came to {{fact:pl_taxable_2024}} in 2024 and {{fact:pl_taxable_2025}} in 2025,
the two lowest years since at least 2018, against {{fact:pl_taxable_fit_range}} a year from 2018
to 2023. (All of 2025's permits add up to more, but the difference is mostly the new Pierce
School and other tax-exempt work, which adds no new growth.) Real estate new growth has come to {{fact:pl_ratio_range}} times taxable permits
about two years later, in the fiscal year numbered three higher (2024's permits feed FY2027), leaving
aside business equipment, which permits do not cover. These point to real estate new growth
worth {{fact:pl_2027_expected}} in FY2027 ({{fact:pl_2027_range}}) and {{fact:pl_2028_expected}} in
FY2028, against {{fact:pl_2027_base}} and {{fact:pl_2028_base}} in the base case. FY2027 is about what
the base case already holds, the Town's budgeted figure; FY2028 falls short even at the high end of
past experience. Permits for new homes were few as well:
{{fact:homes_permits_2024}} net new homes in 2024 and {{fact:homes_permits_2025}} in 2025, against
the {{fact:homes_pace}} a year the base case assumes. If new growth comes in at that level for two
years and then returns to its trend, the room would be {{fact:pl_effect_fy2028}} smaller in FY2028,
leaving {{fact:fy2028_after_permits}} instead of {{fact:room_fy2028}} of room, and {{fact:pl_effect_fy2040}} smaller by
FY2040 ({{fact:pl_effect_fy2040_range}}). The first check
comes this fall, when the Town sets the FY2027 tax rate and the state certifies its new growth.
Permits issued so far in {{fact:pl_partial_year}}, {{fact:pl_partial_to_date}} to
{{fact:pl_last_permit}}, are on pace for {{fact:pl_partial_annualized}}, closer to the earlier years.

## When to revisit

- **Each budget cycle**, when the Town's own long-range plan is updated.
- **When the teachers' contracts settle.** Pay is the largest single number in this forecast.
- **When the district updates its enrollment forecast**, to see whether births and
  kindergarten are following the base case's forecast.
- **Before the FY2031 budget**, when the pension money is freed and its use is decided.

The [Forecast Explorer](@explorer) lets anyone try a different answer to any of these decisions
and see what it does to every year to 2040.
