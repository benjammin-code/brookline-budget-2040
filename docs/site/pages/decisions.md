## What could use up the room, or widen it

Each bar moves one assumption away from the base case and shows the effect on revenue minus
spending in a single year, FY2040 and FY2035. Navy bars are decisions Brookline makes; gray bars
are set mostly outside Brookline; purple bars are about growth. The two years show timing.
Keeping the freed pension money matters as much by FY2035 as by FY2040, while the retiree health
payoff frees money only after {{fact:opeb_paid_off}}. Pay, health premiums and growth compound,
so each grows much larger between the two years.

{{chart:decide}}

- **How fast pay grows.** One point a year faster for all staff, {{fact:pay_avg_up1}} on average
  instead of {{fact:pay_avg}}, costs {{fact:fy40_pay_up1_abs}} a year by FY2040, and the room would
  fall a little short in FY2028 and run out for good from {{fact:runs_out_pay_up1}}. The teachers' contracts expired in August 2026 and are
  being negotiated now.
- **What the health plan costs.** Premiums rising 10% a year instead of 8% from FY2030 cost
  {{fact:fy40_premiums_high_abs}} a year by FY2040. Brookline does not set the rate, but it does
  set its share of the premium and the plan design, in negotiation with its unions.
- **School staffing as enrollment changes.** The base case assumes school staff keep falling
  about half as fast as enrollment, as they did from FY2019 to FY2025, about ten positions a year.
  Holding staff at the FY2027 level instead, as the School Department's own plan does, would cost
  {{fact:fy40_staff_keep_abs}} a year by FY2040, and the room would
  fall a little short in FY2028 and run out for good from {{fact:runs_out_staff_keep}}. Letting enrollment-linked positions follow enrollment fully would
  add {{fact:fy40_staff_follow_abs}}. Holding more staff keeps money in classrooms with fewer
  students, and keeps open seats that children from new homes could fill.
- **The pension money freed after FY2030.** When the pension system reaches full funding, its
  catch-up payments end. The Town plans to move that money to the retiree health liability;
  counting it as room instead would add {{fact:fy40_pension_kept_abs}} a year to the room by FY2040,
  while that liability, about {{fact:opeb_net_fy2027}} in FY2027, kept rising. At $43 million a
  year the liability is paid off in {{fact:opeb_paid_off}} on the actuary's own trend for retiree
  health costs, and the money is free again, {{fact:opeb_freed_fy2040}} a year by FY2040. The base case sets that money aside for a decision then and doesn't count it as room.
  The payoff date rests on that trend: if retirees' costs grew as fast as the base case's 8%
  premiums, the liability would not be paid off before 2040. Counting the money as room would
  leave {{fact:room_fy2040_with_freed}} of room in FY2040 instead of {{fact:room_fy2040}}.

## Growth as a lever

New commercial property pays its own tax, so it raises the Town's total without raising anyone
else's bill. If pay runs a point high, an override to close the gap would add about
{{fact:bill_override_sf_extra_fy2040}} a year to the median single-family bill by FY2040. Growth at
the pace of the fastest-growing fifth of Massachusetts towns through commercial space would avoid it, leaving
{{fact:room_fy2040_pay_up1_commercial}} of room in FY2040; the same growth through new homes alone
would still run out in {{fact:runs_out_pay_up1_housing}}.

## Smaller decisions

Police and fire staffing, capital spending, and zoning also move the budget, by less. Restoring
police and fire to earlier staffing levels would cost {{fact:fy40_ps_restore_abs}} a year by
FY2040. The comprehensive planning process under way will decide where zoning allows more
housing and commercial space, which is where the growth lever is mostly set.

Several of these decisions already have a place in the Select Board's 2030 Roadmap, which calls
for a five-year list of service and funding priorities, the natural place to weigh all four.
