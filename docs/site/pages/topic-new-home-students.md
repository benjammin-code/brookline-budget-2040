Every estimate of what a new home costs the schools rests on one number: how many public school
students the home brings. This page sets out where the forecast's figure, 0.244 students a home,
comes from, checks it against four other sources, and shows what different kinds of building
would bring. How the forecast counts the homes themselves is on
[Counting Brookline's homes](@topic-homes), and how enrollment is forecast is on
[Enrollment: three forecasts compared](@topic-enrollment).

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning on this page |
|---|---|
| Home | A housing unit as the Census counts it: a house, a condominium unit or an apartment, occupied or vacant |
| House | A one-unit building, detached or attached, with no condominium fee, owned or rented |
| Condominium | A home whose owner pays a condominium fee. The survey records the fee only for owners, so a rented condominium counts as an apartment |
| Apartment | A rented home in a building of two or more homes, including the rented half of a two-family house |
| Children | Everyone aged 5 to 17, in public school, private school or neither |
| Public school students | Children enrolled in a public school, kindergarten to grade 12 |

Condominium is a form of ownership, not a kind of building: a three-family house split into
condominiums and a 50-unit condominium building are both condominiums here. That is one reason
this page leans on bedrooms rather than kind of home.

## Bedrooms matter more than the kind of home

The Census Bureau's American Community Survey asks each household about its home (bedrooms,
building size, owned or rented, condominium fee) and about everyone living there, including
whether each child attends public school. Its records for individual households are published only
for areas of at least 100,000 people; the smallest that includes Brookline also covers Dedham and
Milton, and Brookline is about half of it. The 2020 to 2024 records cover about 2,400 homes.

{{chart:students-bedrooms}}

| Bedrooms | Public school students per home | 90% range | Households surveyed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 or 1 | 0.02 | 0.00 to 0.03 | 401 |
| 2 | 0.22 | 0.18 to 0.27 | 554 |
| 3 | 0.37 | 0.32 to 0.42 | 737 |
| 4 or more | 0.57 | 0.49 to 0.66 | 648 |

At the same number of bedrooms, the kinds of home differ by less than their ranges, with two
exceptions. A two-bedroom condominium holds fewer students (0.15) than a two-bedroom house or
apartment (0.26 and 0.23). And four-bedroom homes that are owned hold far more than the few that
are rented.

**Owned and rented.** Up to three bedrooms, rented homes hold as many students as owned ones, or a
few more:

| Bedrooms | Owned | Rented |
|---|---|---|
| 0 or 1 | 0.03 | 0.01 |
| 2 | 0.19 | 0.25 |
| 3 | 0.36 | 0.42 |
| 4 or more | 0.60 | 0.28 |

Only at four or more bedrooms do owners hold more, and only about one rented home in twenty is
that large.

## Brookline's own counts

The survey covers three towns. Two sources cover Brookline alone, and both agree with it.

**Neighborhoods.** The 2010 and 2020 Censuses count children on every block. Brookline's 44
block groups (neighborhoods of about 1,500 people) differ widely in children per home, and the share
of their homes with three or more bedrooms explains most of the difference: the correlation is
0.89 in 2010 and 0.90 in 2020. The share of homes that are owned tracks nearly as closely (0.77 to
0.83), because the two go together in Brookline's neighborhoods, so the block groups alone cannot
say which one matters. The survey above can, because it compares owned and rented homes of the
same size.

Matching each block's homes in the assessors' records to its Census count gives children per home
for each kind (all children 5 to 17, public and private):

| Kind of home | 2010 | 2020 | Homes in 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-family houses | 0.66 | 0.72 | 4,732 |
| Two- and three-family houses | 0.47 | 0.60 | 2,868 |
| Condominiums | 0.11 | 0.18 | 9,712 |
| Apartments and other | 0.14 | 0.18 | 10,753 |
| All homes | 0.27 | 0.33 | 27,961 |

Houses hold the most because they have the most bedrooms. Condominiums with three or more
bedrooms held about 0.34 children in 2020, about 40% more than two-bedroom condominiums (0.24).

**New developments.** The Town and the Schools counted the students enrolled in fall 2025 in
ten developments of eight or more homes built since 2015, not counting age-restricted and Housing
Authority buildings. They range from 0.03 to 0.56 students a home and average {{fact:sph_dev_mean}}.
The eight apartment developments average 0.16 at an average of 1.56 bedrooms, close to what the
survey schedule gives for a typical rental mix (0.16, below). The two townhouse developments, at
three bedrooms, hold 0.25 and 0.56, around the schedule's 0.37.

## How the forecast's 0.244 fits

The forecast's figure was not measured by dividing students by homes. It is the per-bedroom
schedule in RKG Associates' fiscal study for the Town (none for a studio or one-bedroom, 0.25 for
two bedrooms, 0.35 for three) applied to the bedrooms of the 34 homes Brookline built from FY2018
to FY2025, which average 2.31. Students who live outside Brookline, through METCO or by paying
tuition, never enter it. The steps from the Census's count to the forecast's figure:

| Measure | Per home | What it counts |
|---|---|---|
| Census 2020, all homes | 0.33 | Children 5 to 17, in any school or none |
| The same, public school only | about 0.25 | At Brookline's share of children in public school, {{fact:public_share_brookline}} |
| Check: October 2019 enrollment per home | 0.27, or 0.25 without about 500 students from outside Brookline | Independent of the survey |
| Survey schedule, the 34 homes built FY2018 to FY2025 | 0.27, or about 0.245 at Brookline's public school share | New homes, not all homes |
| RKG's schedule, the same 34 homes | 0.244 | The forecast's figure |
| The Town's count, ten developments since 2015 | {{fact:sph_dev_mean}} | Mostly smaller apartments |

Brookline's existing homes and its recent new ones come out close because their bedrooms are
close, about 2.4 and 2.31 on average. The survey schedule runs a little higher than RKG's at three
bedrooms and more, but about 82% of the three towns' children attend public school against
{{fact:public_share_brookline}} of Brookline's, and correcting for that brings it back to about
0.245. The forecast's 0.244 holds up.

## What different buildings would bring

Applying the survey schedule to a building's mix of homes:

| Kind of building | Homes with 0-1 / 2 / 3 / 4+ bedrooms | Students per home | Per 100 homes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mostly studios and one-bedrooms | 80% / 20% / 0 / 0 | 0.06 | 6 |
| Half one-bedroom, half two | 50% / 50% / 0 / 0 | 0.12 | 12 |
| Typical new rental building | 47% / 37% / 13% / 3% | 0.16 | 16 |
| Family-sized mix | 20% / 40% / 30% / 10% | 0.26 | 26 |
| Brookline's homes built FY2018 to FY2025 | averaging 2.31 bedrooms | 0.27 | 27 |
| Three-bedroom townhouses | 0 / 0 / 100% / 0 | 0.37 | 37 |

The first two and the family-sized mix are illustrations, not real buildings. The range is
six-fold, and nearly all of it comes from the share of homes with three or more bedrooms. A
building's average bedrooms is not enough on its own, because students rise faster than bedrooms
above two: a building of all two-bedrooms gives 0.22, while one that is half one-bedrooms and half
three-bedrooms, the same average, gives 0.19. For a single project, the unit mix answers most of
the question.

## How the forecast uses it

The enrollment forecast and the students from new homes are counted separately, so that no family
is counted twice.

**Families moving in, at the recent pace, are in the enrollment forecast.** NESDEC's method turns
births into kindergartners and moves each class up a grade by ratios drawn from Brookline's recent
history, and those ratios already include the families who moved into new homes in those years, as
well as those who moved into older ones. The forecast assumes new homes keep coming at the recent
pace, about {{fact:homes_pace}} a year, and adds no students for them beyond what the enrollment
forecast already holds.

**Faster building adds students on top.** Homes built beyond that pace add 0.244 students each, and
the 266 homes approved at Chestnut Hill, mostly studios and one-bedrooms, add 0.126 each.

**A check on the recent pace.** NESDEC's ratios grow a class about 2% from kindergarten to grade 12.
That sits between the years before COVID, when classes shrank about 7%, and the years since, when
they grew about 6%, so it leans on the recent years. Those were busy years for building: permits
issued in 2019 to 2022, finished as those classes moved up, came to about 155 homes a year not
counting age-restricted ones, against about 43 a year in 2012 to 2018. The forecast's pace of
{{fact:homes_pace}} a year includes age-restricted homes, about 77 a year without them. So NESDEC's
ratios probably carry somewhat more children from new homes than the forecast's pace of building
will bring, perhaps 5 to 20 students a year, or roughly 60 to 250 by FY2040, 1% to 5% of
enrollment. If anything, that puts the base case's enrollment a little high, not low. The ratios
also include families returning after COVID, which this check cannot separate.

## What could change it

- **Families may keep filling homes faster.** Children per home rose from 0.27 to 0.33 between 2010
  and 2020, and none of the rise came from building different kinds of homes: houses, condominiums
  and apartments each held more children. A schedule measured in 2020 to 2024 could run high or low
  by the 2030s. This is the largest uncertainty, and it works through the enrollment forecast's
  rate of families moving in more than through new homes. The Forecast Explorer's *Families moving
  in* setting tests it: each class 0.35 point a year larger, the rate since COVID, brings
  {{fact:enroll_fy2040_families_recent_change}} students by FY2040, and 1 point a year larger
  brings {{fact:enroll_fy2040_families_more_change}}
  ([Assumptions register](@assumptions#families-moving-in)). As Brookline's owners age, more
  family-sized homes should change hands, which points toward the first of these
  ([Family-sized homes changing hands](@topic-homes-changing-hands)).
- **Brookline is not the three towns.** Brookline's single-family houses hold more children than
  the survey's three-town figure for houses (0.72 against 0.61 children 5 to 17), its condominiums
  fewer (0.18 against 0.27), and its families send a smaller share to public school. These pull in
  different directions, which is why the schedule is checked above against Brookline's own counts.
- **New buildings fill slowly.** The ten developments were counted once, and several are only a
  few years old. Families with school-age children may arrive as tenants turn over.
- **The ten developments are averaged as equals.** A building of 8 homes and one of 250 count the
  same in their {{fact:sph_dev_mean}}. Weighting them by homes, and comparing each with what the
  schedule predicts for its own mix of homes, needs each building's bedrooms, which are being
  gathered from building permits and plans.
