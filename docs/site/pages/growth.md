## What growth adds

Each year's building permits become new taxable value about two years later, when the work is
first assessed, so revenue from growth can be seen coming
([revenue follows construction](@topic-growth-links#revenue-follows-construction-about-two-years-later)).
Most of it is not new homes. Renovation of existing homes, including houses rebuilt as houses,
added more assessed value from FY2018 to FY2025 than all the buildings that added homes, and
commercial property and business equipment make up much of the rest
([what new homes add, and what renovation adds](@topic-growth-links#what-new-homes-add-and-what-renovation-adds)).
Those add tax with little or no added cost.

What is built matters as much as how much. Reaching the growth rate of the fastest-growing fifth
of Massachusetts towns would leave the budget {{fact:fy40_peer_commercial_abs}} a year better off
by FY2040, after the costs the growth brings, if it came through commercial space, and
{{fact:fy40_peer_housing_abs}} if it came through new homes alone. New homes bring students and
Town services as well as tax, so less of their tax is left over.
[What new growth is worth](@topic-growth-value#what-new-growth-is-worth) shows the arithmetic,
type by type.

New homes still pay their way. At the recent pace, the homes the forecast adds bring in
{{fact:homes_net_fy2040}} a year more in FY2040 than their students and Town services cost, so
building fewer would not save money. How much a home adds depends on its kind: a condominium adds
more than it costs, and an apartment in a large building about breaks even
([by type of home](@topic-growth#by-type-of-home)).

## What growth costs

A fiscal estimate for a single project charges each new home an average share of what the Town
spends: teachers, police, firefighters. Brookline's costs have not moved that way. From FY2011 to
FY2025 its permits added {{fact:homes_permitted_fy11_25}} homes while police and fire staffing fell
by five positions, and school enrollment rose and fell with births and with families moving into
existing homes far more than with new construction.

Costs arrive when a service runs out of room: a grade at its class-size limit, or a new fire
company. That depends on everything being built across town, not on any one project.

## Is there room?

By some measures, Brookline's services seem to have capacity. The schools have about 1,100 open
seats in kindergarten to grade 8 under the School Committee's own class-size guidelines, about 440
if every class is kept three students below them, and enrollment is expected to keep falling. Fire companies run about half as often as the usual mark
of a busy company, and police calls are down.

Those measures come from budgets and reports. The people who run these services have not been
asked, and they may see it differently. Services often strain before any measure says they are
full: through overtime and leave, in one school or grade, or in the neighborhood farthest from a
station. Where each one's limit lies is not known.

## How to judge a project

The useful questions are town-wide:

- **Is it part of the pace the forecast already counts?** The base case assumes
  {{fact:homes_pace}} net new homes a year, mostly apartments and condominiums.
- **Does it use scarce room?** A family-sized building near a full school uses scarcer room than
  one near a school with open seats.
- **Does it bring a step closer?** With everything else being built, does it bring the town
  nearer a new fire company or a new classroom?

[New homes and Brookline's budget](@new-homes) works through what a new home adds when its
children fill open seats, and [New homes and the budget: is there room?](@topic-growth-links)
sets out the evidence, service by service, and what to watch.
