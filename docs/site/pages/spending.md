## Why costs keep outrunning the cap

Since 2005 Brookline's operating spending has grown about **3.65% a year**, against the cap's
2.5%. (These pages use two periods. Totals of revenue and spending go back to FY2005, twenty
years that include four overrides. Spending sorted by cause starts in FY2011, the first year after
the state changed how towns report spending by category, so earlier years cannot be compared line
by line; the forecast continues the FY2011 to FY2025 record for the same reason.) Two parts of the budget account for nearly all of the difference:

- **Employee benefits**, pensions and health insurance for current and retired staff, grew
  about 5.5% a year.
- **Schools** grew about 4.5% a year. Enrollment rose by almost a third from 2005 to 2019, and
  when it fell back, staff fell by less.

Everything else the Town does, from police and fire to libraries and public works, grew about
2.5% a year, close to what the cap allows.

Sorted by cause, most of that growth is not more people but the same staff costing more.

## Where the $177 million went

Every dollar of the {{fact:spending_growth_fy11_25}} increase in Brookline's yearly General Fund
spending, from FY2011 to FY2025, can be assigned to one of nine causes. The colors show who sets
each one.

{{chart:causes}}

**Inflation is the largest piece by far.** The same staff doing the same work cost more each
year as pay, prices and health premiums rose. Schools carry the largest share of it because they
are the largest part of the budget.

Inflation here is measured with published benchmarks rather than Brookline's own costs: salaries
grow with the national index of state and local government pay ({{fact:infl_eci_fy11_25}} a year
from FY2011 to FY2025), other purchases with Boston-area consumer prices
({{fact:infl_cpi_fy11_25}}), and health premiums with the national index of public-sector benefit
costs ({{fact:infl_benefits_fy11_25}}). Anything Brookline's cost per person did beyond those
benchmarks is counted separately, as "Pay & costs vs inflation", and it came to only
{{fact:cause_pay_costs_vs_inflation}}. Brookline's costs for the same staff and supplies rose
almost exactly as fast as public employers' costs nationally. Schools ran a little above the
benchmarks ({{fact:pay_vs_infl_schools}}) and Town departments a little below
({{fact:pay_vs_infl_town}}). Health premiums added {{fact:pay_vs_infl_health_infl}} and matched the
national index almost to the dollar ({{fact:pay_vs_infl_health}}). The rest
({{fact:pay_vs_infl_pension_rate}}) is pensions costing more per dollar of pay, after the
retirement system changed its actuarial assumptions.

**Buildings come next.** Debt service rose from $9.5 million to $36.3 million a year, mostly for
projects voters approved separately: the Devotion (now Ruffin Ridley), High School, Driscoll and
Pierce schools, the Newbury College land and fire station renovations. Most of that is paid by
[[debt exclusion|debt exclusions]], outside the [[levy limit]], so it grew the budget without
taking room from day-to-day services. [What this means for your tax bill](@tax-bill) explains how
those payments reach a household.

**Then staffing levels, mostly in the schools.** Brookline had 6.75 students per school staff
member in FY2011 and 5.62 in FY2025. Had staff moved one-for-one with enrollment, the schools
would have about 208 fewer positions today, about $19.6 million a year in salaries.

**Then the pension catch-up.** Paying down the pension shortfall, for pensions already earned,
added {{fact:cause_past_pension_shortfall}}. Those payments follow a fixed schedule that reaches
full funding in FY2030, when the money they use is freed for another purpose. [Liabilities, reserves and the credit
rating](@topic-liabilities) has the detail, including retiree health.

## How the school staffing ratio changed

Comparing only FY2011 and FY2025 hides how it happened, because enrollment rose and then fell:

{{chart:staff-enroll}}

From FY2011 to FY2019 enrollment rose 18.5% and staff rose 34%. After COVID, enrollment fell
10.6% by FY2025 while staff fell 5.0%. About 150 of the positions beyond enrollment were added while enrollment was rising; measured against today's smaller enrollment, about 135 of them remain, and about 73 more are positions kept after it fell. Over the same years
the share of students with an individualized education program rose from 15.8% to 18.1%, and
spending per pupil grew fastest in pupil services and counseling. Neither proves why the ratio
changed, but both show where the added spending went. [Schools: staffing, costs and
open seats](@topic-schools) has the detail.

## How much of this Brookline controls

{{chart:who-sets}}

Brookline's own decisions are staffing levels, borrowing, retiree health prefunding, and pay above
or below the national benchmark. Outside Brookline means inflation in pay, prices and premiums,
plus state, county and MBTA assessments. Past obligations are the pension shortfall payments.

The lines between these groups are judgment calls. Pay rates are bargained locally, so Brookline
has some say over the part filed under inflation too; this page counts only the part of pay
growth that differs from the national public-sector benchmark as a local decision. The pension
shortfall built up over decades of earlier decisions, and its payment schedule is now fixed.

## Revenue: what Brookline controls, and what it does not

Over the twenty years to 2025, the revenue that arrives without a vote grew about **3.52% a
year**, just short of costs. Split it by who sets it and the two halves behave very differently.

**The part Brookline controls kept pace.** The levy limit with Brookline's own new growth, and
no overrides, grew about 3.91% a year, faster than costs. Fees the Town sets grew about 3.49%.

**The part it does not control fell behind.** [[State aid]] grew about 1.99% a year; it fell
after 2008 and took until about 2015 to recover. Had it kept pace with costs, it would have been
$34.4 million in 2025 instead of $24.9 million. That $9.5 million shortfall is **87% of the
gap** between costs and what revenue would have raised without overrides, and it is not unique to Brookline: unrestricted local aid across Massachusetts has fallen
about 25% in real terms since 2002.

{{chart:control}}

Brookline should keep pressing the state, but a plan that depends on winning that argument is
not a plan. The levers Brookline controls have to do more.

{{chart:waterfall|Every dollar of growth in what Brookline may collect, FY2005 to FY2025, by where it came from. The bottom band is property that already existed in 2005; the three bands above it are the only ways the total can rise.}}

Of the $175 million Brookline's allowed tax revenue grew over those twenty years, the automatic
2.5% supplied $75 million, new growth $61 million and overrides $39 million.

## What this means for the forecast

The same causes drive the forecast. Pay and health premium growth decide the largest piece; the
school staffing ratio decides much of the rest; and new homes add relatively little cost.
[Will Brookline need another override?](@budget-to-2040) puts a number on each.
