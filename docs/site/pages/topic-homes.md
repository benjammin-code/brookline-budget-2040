## Five ways of counting

Each source answers a slightly different question.

| Source | What it counts | Period | Homes a year | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Town building permits, read one by one for this project | Homes created less homes demolished, by the year the permit was issued | 2008 to 2025 | {{fact:homes_pace}} since 2016; {{fact:homes_pace_2008_15}} in 2008 to 2015 | Dated when work was permitted, not finished; a few permits do not state how many homes |
| Brookline Assessors, yearly rolls | Housing units recorded on each property, each January 1 | January {{fact:gl_au_from}} to January {{fact:gl_au_to}} | {{fact:gl_au_per_year}}, once cleaned | The unit field begins with the FY2017 roll and changed definition in FY2026; record fixes look like new homes |
| Planning Department, building permits | Net new homes permitted | Calendar 2022 to 2024 | 166 (252, 190, 57) | Three years only, including the busiest in two decades |
| US Census, decennial count | Every housing unit, occupied or not | April 2010 to April 2020 | about 150 ({{fact:gl_homes_2010}} to {{fact:gl_homes_2020}}) | Only every ten years; does not match the permits (below) |
| Census Bureau, building permit survey | Homes in newly built buildings only | 2006 to 2019 | about 30 | Misses conversions and homes added inside existing buildings |

**The permits.** The Town's permit files from 2008 hold about 1,700 to 2,500 building permits a
year. Every one that could create or remove a home was read and coded: homes created and
demolished, the kind of building, and the project it belongs to, so a project with several
permits counts once. Assisted living is kept separate, since it brings no school children. By the
year each project's main permit was issued, net of demolitions and without assisted living:

| Year | 2008 | 2009 | 2010 | 2011 | 2012 | 2013 | 2014 | 2015 | 2016 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net new homes | 81 | 16 | 3 | 30 | 55 | 19 | 49 | 72 | 28 |

| Year | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net new homes | 15 | 65 | 172 | 189 | 108 | 262 | 150 | 34 | 24 |

The step up from 2016 came with the large rental buildings approved under the state's
comprehensive permit law (Chapter 40B). Assisted living adds about 150 more, nearly all of it
Fisher Hill in 2022.

**The Planning Department's 166.** Its June 2026 development look-ahead counted 252, 190 and 57
net new homes in permits issued in 2022, 2023 and 2024, 499 in all, or 166 a year. The count
here comes to 262, 150 and 34 for the same years, 446 in all. The differences most likely come
from which permits are netted against the homes they replaced (one Housing Authority building's
115 senior apartments replaced 60 older ones) and from two 2024 permits that do not state
their number of homes. Matching it exactly would take the Planning Department's project list. The
three years are also unusual: 2022 was the busiest year in two decades.

**The assessors' rolls.** They need cleaning before they can be read as new homes. Their total
rose {{fact:gl_au_raw}} from January {{fact:gl_au_from}} to January {{fact:gl_au_to}}, but much of
that was records rather than building: hotel rooms recorded as homes, a condominium parking lot,
and the first unit counts for several tax-exempt housing complexes that had stood for decades.
Without those, and with assisted living counted separately, the rolls show {{fact:gl_au_net}} more
homes, arriving unevenly. Of the projects permitted from 2016 to mid-2023, all but one show up on
the rolls, a median of about ten months after the permit, and permits issued from 2015 to 2022 net
1,067 homes against 1,050 on the rolls, counting assisted living in both.

**The Census.** The two Census counts differ by about 1,500 homes, about 150 a year, but the
permits that could have been finished by April 2020 add up to {{fact:homes_permits_09_18}}, and the
federal permit survey agrees. The gap most likely lies in how the two Censuses counted rather than
in building nobody permitted. This site uses the Census only to compare Brookline with other
towns, which only the Census allows; for Brookline's own record it uses the permits.

## What kind of homes were added

Net new homes in permits issued from 2016 to 2025, without assisted living, with each demolition
counted against the project that replaced it:

| Kind of home | Net new homes | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Rental apartment buildings, mostly approved under Chapter 40B | 538 | 51% |
| Age-restricted apartments | 214 | 20% |
| Condominiums and small multifamily buildings | 123 | 12% |
| Houses (single, two and three-family) | 129 | 12% |
| Conversions and accessory units | 45 | 4% |
| **Total** | **1,049** | |
| Assisted living, counted separately | 151 | |

The houses include 44 townhouses that are part of a rental Chapter 40B development; without
them, houses added about 85 homes in ten years.

Houses add value but barely add homes. Of the 264 new single-family houses permitted from 2008 to
2025, most replaced a house that was torn down. A house rebuilt
as a house counts here, and in the forecast, as renovation; a house rebuilt as a two-family adds
one home.

Homes still to come are harder to count. The Town's 2024 Housing Plan lists 1,221 homes approved
since 2016 through Chapter 40B. As of February 2024, 396 were built, 473 under construction and
352 not yet started, and none of the not-yet-started projects had a large building permit by
September 2026. Recent permits are few: {{fact:homes_permits_2024}} net new homes in 2024 and
{{fact:homes_permits_2025}} in 2025, well under the forecast's {{fact:homes_pace}} a year. Since
homes reach the roll about a year after their permits, the next two years will very likely fall
short of that pace, and the permits for all construction point the same way for new growth
([Navigating the next few years](@next-few-years#what-to-watch-and-what-each-reading-tells-you)).

## What each kind of home adds to the tax base

Each project permitted from 2015 to 2022 that added homes, followed onto the assessment rolls
through FY2026: the increase in its assessed value on each roll after its permit, net of market
change and in FY2026 dollars, counting increases only. That is how state new growth is measured,
parcel by parcel against the year before; a demolition does not reduce it.

| Kind of home | Projects | Net new homes | Value added | Per net new home |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apartment buildings | 3 | 334 | $174M | about $521,000 |
| Condominiums and small multifamily buildings | 6 | 46 | $40M | about $864,000 |
| Conversions and accessory units | 36 | 50 | $80M | about $1.6 million |
| Houses that added a home | 73 | 116 | $447M | about $3.9 million |
| Age-restricted apartments | 2 | 112 | $16M | about $146,000 |
| Assisted living (Fisher Hill) | 1 | 151 | $74M | about $490,000 |
| **All** | | **809** | **$831M** | **about $1.0 million** |

A house that adds a home carries the whole new building's value, which is why houses come to
several million dollars per home added. Age-restricted homes add little: one of the two buildings
measured is affordable senior housing and the other was not finished, and a third, a Housing Authority
building, pays no tax. The apartment and condominium samples are small,
and large buildings take two to four years to reach their full value on the roll, so these are
best read as rounded figures.

## What the forecast assumes

**How many.** The forecast keeps total residential new growth at its recent rate, about 0.84% of
the levy a year. Since 2009 that rate has stayed between 0.74% and 1.02%, whether about 40 or
about 180 homes a year were being built: when fewer homes are built, renovation fills in. Within
that total, the forecast counts {{fact:homes_pace}} net new homes a year, the permits' pace since
2016, plus the 266 approved at Chestnut Hill, about {{fact:homes_fy2040}} homes in all by FY2040.
The rest of residential new growth is renovation, including houses rebuilt as houses.
The Forecast Explorer's *New homes built* setting tests fewer: half the expected new homes, which
leaves {{fact:fy40_homes_half_abs}} less room in FY2040, or none at all, with renovation and
commercial growth at their trend, {{fact:fy40_homes_none_abs}} less. That setting does not let
renovation fill in for the missing homes, so it shows the most they could cost the budget.

**What kind.** The homes the forecast adds are apartments and condominiums, in their 2016 to 2025
proportions, about four to one. It does not forecast more age-restricted homes, and houses and
conversions, which mostly rebuild homes that were already there, sit with renovation. Each forecast
home adds about {{fact:homes_value}} of assessed value, rising with home values after FY2026, and
brings 0.244 school-age children, from the per-bedroom rates in RKG Associates' fiscal study for
the Town applied to the bedrooms of recent new homes.

**What would change it.** A different mix changes both sides of the budget. A family-sized home
pays several times the tax of an apartment and brings more children. A zoning change that lets
new houses go up on split lots, with nothing torn down, would add a kind of home Brookline has
hardly built; testing it would mean counting what each new home replaces as well as what it adds.

## Which figure the site uses where

- **{{fact:homes_pace}} a year** is the forecast's pace and the permits' since 2016.
- **{{fact:homes_permitted_fy11_25}} homes** is what the permits added from FY2011 to FY2025, used
  when comparing homes with Town staffing.
- **166 a year** is the Planning Department's count for 2022 to 2024, cited only as theirs.
- **About 150 a year** is the Census decade, used only for comparisons with other towns.
