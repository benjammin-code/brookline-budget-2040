Each entry follows the same pattern. **Base case** is what the forecast assumes. **Why** is the
evidence. **If it is wrong** is the change in FY2040 [[room]], revenue minus spending, when that
one assumption moves and everything else stays at the base case; a minus sign means less room.
**Change it** names the setting in the [Forecast Explorer](@explorer).

## Pay

### Town staff pay
- **Base case:** 3.0% a year, the Town's bargaining target.
- **Why:** It is the figure the Town bargains toward.
- **If it is wrong:** one point higher for every group of staff, {{fact:fy40_pay_up1}}; half a
  point lower for every group, {{fact:fy40_pay_down_half}}.
- **Change it:** "Pay raises compared with the base case".

### How much of each budget is pay
- **Base case:** Pay is 83% of the school budget and 76% of Town department spending. Among
  Town departments it is 87% of Public Safety, about 85% of Human Services, about 80% of General
  Government, 63% of Public Works and 52% of Culture and Recreation. Pay grows at each group's
  rate; the rest of each budget grows with prices.
- **Why:** Budgeted salaries against actual spending, FY2019 and FY2026; the shares barely moved
  between the two years.

### School pay
- **Base case:** 3.7% a year, 0.7 points above the Town target.
- **Why:** The current teachers' contract gives 2.75% raises. The School Department's own budget
  arithmetic adds step and degree-column increases and subtracts savings from turnover, which
  comes to about 3.3% to 4.1% a year per position; 3.7% is the middle of that range. The
  FY2011 to FY2025 record was about 3.4%. The contract expired in August 2026 and is being
  negotiated.
- **If it is wrong:** at the School Department's own forecast, about 5% a year,
  {{fact:fy40_school_pay_psb}}.
- **Change it:** "School pay above the Town target".

### Public Safety pay
- **Base case:** 3.5% a year, 0.5 points above the Town target, plus the increases already
  agreed: new fire steps from FY2028, estimated at $909,471 a year, and four police positions
  refilled with override money, two in FY2028 and two in FY2029.
- **Why:** Police and fire cost per position grew about 3.3% a year from FY2011 to FY2025, on
  flat or falling headcount. The faster rise from FY2021 to FY2025 came mostly from one-time
  changes, a police stipend rising from 4% to 7.5% of pay and new steps, which are already in
  the FY2027 budget. Contract raises themselves have run 1.5% to 3%.
- **If it is wrong:** school and Public Safety pay both held to the 3.0% target,
  {{fact:fy40_pay_to_target}}.
- **Change it:** "Public Safety pay above the Town target".

### Police and fire staffing
- **Base case:** the FY2027 budget, 128 sworn police officers and 160 firefighters, with the
  four police positions to be refilled in FY2028 and FY2029 (see Public Safety pay), plus a fire
  company of 20 firefighters, at {{fact:fire_company_cost_fy25}} a year in FY2025 dollars, once
  the town reaches about {{fact:fire_company_homes}} homes. At the recent pace of building that
  comes after FY2040, so the base case adds none; faster growth reaches it sooner. Police cost grows a little with each new home, through the Town
  cost of serving a new home (below).
- **Why:** Fire staffing was 161 from FY2010 to FY2026 and police and fire positions together fell
  by five from FY2011 to FY2025 while the town's building permits added {{fact:homes_permitted_fy11_25}}
  homes. Fire companies are staffed
  around the clock, so fire staffing changes in steps. The threshold is the number of homes at
  which Brookline's firefighters per 1,000 homes would fall to the median of 21 nearby cities
  and towns (5.16); the cost is the Fire Department's own estimate for a South Brookline ladder
  company, health insurance and overtime included. When the departments run below their
  budgeted numbers, the money not spent returns as free cash, which the forecast already counts.
  Police calls fell about 4% a year from FY2016 to FY2024, and Brookline's officers per 1,000
  homes (4.58 in 2019) are close to the same towns' median ({{fact:police_peer_per_1000}}), so
  the same test gives no clear point at which police would step up.
- **If it is wrong:** police back to 136 and fire back to 168, their levels as recently as
  FY2018 to FY2020 and in FY2004, {{fact:fy40_ps_restore}}; fire cost growing a little with every home instead of in one
  company step, which the base case doesn't reach before FY2040, {{fact:fy40_no_fire_company}}; police growing with homes to hold the median,
  {{fact:police_added_fy2040}} more officers by FY2040, {{fact:fy40_police_step}}.
- **Change it:** "Police and fire staffing".

## Benefits

### Health premiums
- **Base case:** 12% a year in FY2028 and FY2029, then 8% a year.
- **Why:** Recent increases have run 10% to 12%; the 25-year average is about 6.5%. The base case
  settles between the two.
- **If it is wrong:** 10% a year from FY2030, {{fact:fy40_premiums_high}}; 6.5%,
  {{fact:fy40_premiums_low}}; 4.5%, the actuary's long-run trend for retiree health,
  {{fact:fy40_premiums_actuary}}. These settings move the health insurance line; the retiree
  health liability follows the actuary's own trend (below).
- **Change it:** "Health premium growth, FY2030 on" and "Health premium growth, FY2028 and
  FY2029".

### Who is on the health plan
- **Base case:** Active employees follow staffing. Retirees, about 36% of the plan's cost, grow
  0.92% a year on their own.
- **Why:** Retirees and their survivors are about half of the plan's subscribers (1,659 of 3,390
  in FY2025), but many are on cheaper Medicare supplement plans: the FY2024 audit's payments for
  retirees, $12.4 million, were 36% of that year's group health spending, $34.8 million. Retiree
  subscribers grew from 1,459 in FY2011 to 1,659 in FY2025.

### Pensions and retiree health
- **Base case:** The pension system reaches full funding in FY2030, on the Town's funding
  schedule. From FY2031 the Town pays $43.0 million a year into the retiree health ([[OPEB]])
  trust, as its plan does, on top of retirees' premiums, until the unfunded liability is paid
  off. Then the money is free again; the base case sets it aside for a decision then and doesn't
  count it as room. The pension cost that remains after full
  funding grows 0.65 point a year faster than Town pay, 3.65% in the base case: the actuary's 3.5%
  payroll growth plus 0.15 point for longer lives. Other benefits follow the payroll of all staff,
  Town and schools. The liability is rolled forward from the
  FY2024 audit on the actuary's own trend for retiree health costs, about 5.9% a year in FY2026
  falling to 4.5% by FY2035. On that trend it rises to about {{fact:opeb_net_fy2030}} in FY2030 and
  is paid off in {{fact:opeb_paid_off}}. If retirees' costs grew instead with the base case's
  health premiums, 8% a year, it would not be paid off before 2040.
- **Why:** Both are the Town's own adopted schedule and policy, and the retiree health trend is
  the actuary's. The audited record supports it: from FY2017 to FY2024 the total liability, before subtracting the trust, barely moved,
  from $292 million to $302 million, and payments to retirees rose about 1% a year.
- **If it is wrong:** keeping the freed pension money in the budget instead,
  {{fact:fy40_pension_kept}}; counting the money freed by the retiree health payoff as room,
  {{fact:fy40_opeb_spend}}. Full funding arriving two years late barely moves the room:
  FY2030's last catch-up payment is spread over the extra
  years, paid from the money set aside for retiree health, so that liability is paid down later.
- **Change it:** "Pension savings after full funding" and "Pension full funding slips".

## Schools

### School staffing
- **Base case:** School staff fall half as fast as enrollment, from about 1,242 in FY2027 to
  about 1,110 by FY2040, while enrollment falls about a fifth. Students from new homes are staffed
  in full.
- **Why:** It is what Brookline did: from FY2019 to FY2025 enrollment fell 10.6% and staff fell
  5.0%. That is about ten fewer positions a year, which retirements and departures would likely
  cover.
- **If it is wrong:** staff held at the FY2027 level, as the School Department's FY2027 to FY2029
  plan does, {{fact:fy40_staff_keep}}, and the room falls a little short in FY2028 and runs out for good from {{fact:runs_out_staff_keep}}; staff
  follow enrollment down fully, {{fact:fy40_staff_follow}}.
- **Change it:** "School staff as enrollment falls".

### Enrollment
- **Base case:** [[NESDEC]]'s forecast, scaled to the actual counts for fall 2025 and fall 2026
  and updated for actual births, falling to {{fact:enroll_fy2040_about}} by FY2040.
- **Why:** The decline follows births. Children entering kindergarten through fall 2029 are
  already born, and resident births fell from about 590 a year in 2015 to 2019 to 422 in 2023
  and 443 in 2024. NESDEC's 2023 forecast had estimated 518 and 507 for those two years, so the
  base case scales each of those classes, and later ones, down to the births that actually
  happened, holding births from 2025 at the 2022 to 2024 average, {{fact:births_ahead}} a year.
- **Alternative:** The School Department's own forecast ([[Cropper-McKibben]]) has enrollment
  rising again to about 7,400 by FY2034, mostly because it projects more births (about 600 a year).
  If births stay near the counted level, that enrollment would take about
  {{fact:cm_per_birth_fy2034}} students for every resident birth, against a twenty-year high of 0.90.
- **If it is wrong:** enrollment rebounds as the School Department forecasts, and staff rise with
  it, {{fact:fy40_enroll_cm_staff}}. (Staff fall half as fast as enrollment only while it falls;
  students added when it rises are staffed in full.)
- **More:** [Enrollment: three forecasts compared](@topic-enrollment).
- **Change it:** "School enrollment forecast".

### School costs other than staff
- **Base case:** Grow with prices.
- **If it is wrong:** two points a year faster than prices, {{fact:fy40_school_np_extra}}.
- **Change it:** "School non-personnel costs above inflation".

## Prices and capital

### Inflation
- **Base case:** 2.5% a year for everything the Town buys other than staff.
- **If it is wrong:** 3.5% a year, {{fact:fy40_inflation_high}}.
- **More:** [Utilities: in step with prices since 2019](@topic-spending#utilities-in-step-with-prices-since-2019).
- **Change it:** "Inflation".

### Capital
- **Base case:** The Select Board's policy from FY2028: 6.6% of the prior year's net revenue from
  revenue (4.5% for debt, 2.1% in cash), plus free cash. Debt follows the Town's six-year capital
  table through FY2032, and capital paid in cash makes up the rest of the 6.6%.
- **Why:** The FY2027 Financial Plan holds the policy at 6% for FY2026 and FY2027 only, for
  operating relief, and assumes the full 6.6% after that; its own table returns to 6.6% in FY2028.
  The base case applies the share to its own revenue, which counts the override passed after the
  table was written.
- **If it is wrong:** holding capital at the table's dollars, about 6% of this forecast's revenue,
  {{fact:fy40_capital_table}}. The policy has been set aside before, as in FY2026 and FY2027.
- **Change it:** "Capital funding".

### Reserves and other non-departmental lines
- **Base case:** By Town policy. The reserve fund is 1% of the prior year's net revenue. The
  other general lines (the stabilization, liability and housing trust deposits, insurance and
  small administrative items) follow the Town's plan to FY2031 and then grow with net revenue;
  items the Town pays without appropriation, such as the MBTA and county assessments and the
  overlay, grow about 2.4% a year.
- **Why:** These lines follow written policies or state law rather than a trend.

## Growth

### New growth rate
- **Base case:** New growth adds {{fact:trend_growth_rate}} of the levy a year, Brookline's rate
  over FY2022 to FY2026.
- **Why:** It is the recent record. Over FY2005 to FY2025 it averaged 1.37%; the
  fastest-growing fifth of Massachusetts towns reach {{fact:peer_growth_rate}}.
- **If it is wrong:** growth at {{fact:peer_growth_rate}}, half homes and half commercial,
  {{fact:fy40_peer_even}}; led by commercial space, {{fact:fy40_peer_commercial}}; led by new
  homes, {{fact:fy40_peer_housing}}.
- **Change it:** "Property tax new growth" and "Where added growth comes from".

### New growth in FY2027 and FY2028
- **Base case:** FY2027 at the Town's budgeted $2.5 million, the figure in the adopted budget;
  FY2028 at the trend rate, as in every later year.
- **Why:** Building permits show new growth about two years ahead, and the 2024 and 2025 permits
  were the lowest since at least 2018. They point to real estate new growth of
  {{fact:pl_2027_expected}} in FY2027, close to the base case's {{fact:pl_2027_base}}, and
  {{fact:pl_2028_expected}} in FY2028, against {{fact:pl_2028_base}}. The base case keeps the
  trend for FY2028 until the state certifies FY2027's figure this fall.
- **If it is wrong:** FY2028 would have {{fact:fy2028_after_permits}} instead of
  {{fact:room_fy2028}} of room, and {{fact:pl_effect_fy2040}} a year less by FY2040, since
  each year's levy builds on the last.
- **Change it:** "New growth in FY2027 and FY2028" in the Excel workbook; the Explorer does not
  have this setting.

### New homes built
- **Base case:** New homes at the recent pace, {{fact:homes_pace}} a year, about a sixth of new
  growth.
- **If it is wrong:** half as many, {{fact:fy40_homes_half}}; none, with renovation, commercial
  space and business equipment at their pace, {{fact:fy40_homes_none}}. That counts the tax and
  the Town costs of those homes but leaves the enrollment forecast as it is, since it already
  reflects the recent pace of building.
- **Change it:** "New homes built".

### Town cost of serving a new home
- **Base case:** $660 a year (FY2025 dollars) in Town department costs, plus benefits. About a
  third of it, about {{fact:ps_share_per_home}}, is police and fire. The forecast leaves fire's part,
  about {{fact:ps_share_fire_per_home}}, out, because it counts fire in steps instead (see "Police
  and fire staffing" above); police keep their part, about {{fact:ps_share_police_per_home}}.
- **Why:** Comparing all 351 Massachusetts cities and towns from FY2011 to FY2021, those that
  added more homes spent about $660 more on non-school departments for each added home. The
  consultant RKG's average-cost figure for Brookline is $1,109; the low end of the estimates is
  $230.
- **If it is wrong:** $1,109, the consultant RKG's figure for Brookline, which has police and
  fire growing with every home instead of a company at a time: {{fact:fy40_cost_home_rkg}} in
  FY2040 and {{fact:total_cost_home_rkg}} over FY2026 to FY2040, mostly the higher charge for each home; $230, {{fact:fy40_cost_home_low}}.
- **Change it:** "Town cost of serving a new home".

### Students from new homes
- **Base case:** 0.244 school-age children per new home, each one staffed in full at the
  forecast's cost of an added student, {{fact:student_cost}} in FY2027.
- **Why:** 0.244 is a standard per-bedroom schedule applied to the mix of homes Brookline builds;
  ten Brookline buildings opened since 2015 range from 0.03 to 0.56.
- **Alternative:** With enrollment falling, children from new homes can fill seats the schools
  already have, at about $2,477 each for supplies and services rather than new staff.
  [New homes and Brookline's budget](@new-homes) works through that case.
- **If it is wrong:** children from new homes filling empty seats until enrollment is back to the
  FY2027 level, at today's growth rate, {{fact:fy40_fill_seats}};
  growth led by new homes at {{fact:peer_growth_rate}} with filled seats,
  {{fact:fy40_peer_housing_fill}}, against {{fact:fy40_peer_housing}} with new staff.
- **Change it:** "Students from added homes".

### Planning and permitting
- **Base case:** When growth runs faster than the recent rate, Planning and building-inspection
  staff are added in proportion to the extra construction, from $2.6 million a year in the
  FY2027 budget. Slower growth does not cut them.
- **Why:** Permit fees already grow with construction on the revenue side; the staff who review
  it should too.

### Chestnut Hill redevelopment
- **Base case:** The plan Town Meeting approved in May 2026, 266 homes and a 200-room hotel with
  over half the floor area commercial, counted on top of trend growth. Its property tax enters
  the levy a third at a time from FY2032 to FY2034, at each year's value, and then grows with the
  levy; its hotel rooms tax and car excise grow 1.8% a year, like other local receipts.
- **If it is wrong:** counted inside trend growth instead, {{fact:fy40_chca_in_trend}}; at the
  consultant RKG's estimate, {{fact:fy40_chca_rkg}}.
- **Change it:** "Chestnut Hill redevelopment".

## Revenue

### State aid
- **Base case:** Grows 2.5% a year from the adopted FY2027 figure, the rate in the Town's own
  long-range plan.
- **Why:** A conservative choice. State aid grew 3.5% a year from FY2015 to FY2025, but that
  includes a one-year rise of 8.6% in FY2019; without it the rate was about 3.0%. Since FY2019 it
  has grown 2.0% a year, and over twenty years about 2%, with a fall after 2008.
- **Change it:** "State aid growth".

### Local receipts
- **Base case:** Fees, excise and other local receipts grow 1.8% a year, their FY2019 to FY2025
  record with investment income set aside. Meals tax and building permits are forecast
  separately, and investment income is held at the Town's FY2027 budget of about $0.9 million a
  year.
- **Why:** Interest on the Town's cash rose from $1.3 million in FY2019 to $5.1 million in FY2025
  as interest rates rose, nearly half the growth in all local receipts over those years. Part of
  that cash was federal pandemic aid waiting to be spent, which runs out in December 2026, and
  the Town's long-range plan expects interest income to fall as it is spent. Counted in the
  trend, it would carry a peak year forward; with it, receipts grew 3.3% a year.
- **Change it:** "Local receipts growth".

### Free cash
- **Base case:** Each year's surplus comes back as [[free cash]] two years later, as it does in
  practice, and about 80% of it is appropriated. Following the Town's free cash policy, what is
  left after the capital and reserve uses already counted goes to capital and one-time uses, not
  the operating budget, so it is not counted as room.
- **Why:** It is the policy. The Town has at times used this money for operating costs; the base
  case does not.

## Starting point and scope

### Starting point
- **Base case:** The FY2027 budget Town Meeting adopted in May 2026, with the override. Each part
  of the budget moves with its own drivers from FY2028.
- **Why:** The years already budgeted are known. As a check, the forecast reproduces the adopted,
  balanced budgets, and its method, run over FY2011 to FY2025 with what actually happened to
  staffing, enrollment and prices, came within about 4% of Town department spending and 6% for
  schools.

### Votes
- **Base case:** The May 2026 override phases in over FY2027 to FY2029. No further overrides or
  debt exclusions.
- **Change it:** "A further override" and "First year of that override" add one.

### Debt exclusions
- **Base case:** Set aside on both sides. A [[debt exclusion]] is tax that matches specific
  building debt dollar for dollar, so leaving both in would add the same amount to revenue and
  spending without changing the room. Existing payments stay about level through FY2040.
